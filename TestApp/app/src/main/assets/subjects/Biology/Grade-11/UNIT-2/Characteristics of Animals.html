Characteristics of Animals

Study notes, key terminology, examples, revision questions and answers

In this lesson:
Learning outcomes General features Body organization Animal groups Importance Revision questions

1. Learning outcomes

After studying this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Describe the main characteristics of animals.
  • Explain how animals differ from plants and fungi.
  • Use symmetry, body layers, body cavities and segmentation to describe animal body plans.
  • Distinguish major invertebrate groups from vertebrates.
  • Explain the ecological and economic importance of animals.

2. General characteristics of animals

2.1 Multicellular and eukaryotic

Animals are multicellular organisms. Their cells contain a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, so they are eukaryotes. In most animals, cells are organized into tissues, organs and organ systems.

2.2 Heterotrophic nutrition

Animals cannot manufacture all their food from inorganic materials through photosynthesis. They obtain organic nutrients by eating other organisms or organic matter. Food is generally ingested and digested internally.

2.3 No cell walls

Animal cells lack the rigid cellulose cell walls typical of plants. Their cell membranes and extracellular materials help provide structure while allowing cells to move and change shape.

2.4 Movement

Most animals can move from place to place at least during one stage of their life cycle. Muscles and nervous systems support movement and coordination in many groups. Adult sponges, however, are usually attached to a surface.

2.5 Response to stimuli

Animals respond to environmental changes such as light, temperature, touch and chemicals. Many have sensory cells and nervous systems that coordinate responses. Not every animal has a brain or a complex nervous system.

2.6 Growth and development

Animals begin life through development from an embryo. In many groups, embryonic development includes a blastula stage, a characteristic early stage formed from a group of cells. Growth may continue throughout life or slow after maturity.

2.7 Reproduction

Sexual reproduction is common in animals and involves the fusion of male and female gametes to form a zygote. Some animals also reproduce asexually, for example by budding or fragmentation. Fertilization may be internal or external.

2.8 Storage carbohydrate

Animals commonly store carbohydrate as glycogen. Plants commonly store carbohydrate as starch. This is a useful difference, although no single feature should be used alone to classify an organism.

Remember: Animals are eukaryotic, multicellular and heterotrophic, and their cells lack cell walls. Some familiar features, such as active movement and a nervous system, are not present in every animal group.

3. Animal body plans

3.1 Levels of organization

  • Cellular level: cells perform different functions but are not organized into true tissues; sponges are an example.
  • Tissue level: similar cells work together as tissues; this occurs in cnidarians.
  • Organ level: different tissues combine to form organs.
  • Organ-system level: organs work together in systems such as digestion, circulation and respiration.

3.2 Body symmetry

TypeDescriptionExample
AsymmetryNo regular plane divides the body into similar halves.Many sponges
Radial symmetryBody parts are arranged around a central axis; several planes can produce similar halves.Adult sea stars; jellyfish
Bilateral symmetryOne main plane divides the body into right and left halves.Earthworms; insects; humans

Bilateral symmetry is often associated with directional movement and a head region where sensory structures are concentrated. This concentration is called cephalization.

3.3 Germ layers

During embryonic development, germ layers give rise to tissues and organs.

  • Ectoderm: the outer layer; contributes to the epidermis and nervous system in many animals.
  • Endoderm: the inner layer; contributes to the lining of the digestive tract and associated organs.
  • Mesoderm: the middle layer; contributes to muscles and many internal tissues and organs.

Diploblastic animals develop mainly from two germ layers, ectoderm and endoderm. Triploblastic animals develop three, including mesoderm.

3.4 Body cavity

A body cavity is a space between the body wall and internal organs. In the traditional introductory classification of triploblastic animals:

  • Acoelomates: lack a fluid-filled body cavity between the digestive tract and body wall; flatworms are an example.
  • Pseudocoelomates: have a body cavity that is not completely lined by mesoderm; roundworms are a traditional example.
  • Coelomates: have a true coelom completely lined by mesoderm-derived tissue; annelids and vertebrates are examples.

3.5 Segmentation

Segmentation means that the body is divided into repeated sections. It is seen in earthworms, arthropods and vertebrates, although the arrangement and development of segments differ among these groups.

4.1 Invertebrates and vertebrates

Invertebrates are animals without a vertebral column. They include insects, worms, molluscs and many other groups. Vertebrates are chordates with a vertebral column. Their major familiar groups include fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

Do not confuse “invertebrate” with a single phylum: it is a broad informal grouping of animals from many different evolutionary lineages.

5. Importance of animals

  • Ecological: animals pollinate plants, disperse seeds, recycle nutrients and maintain food webs.
  • Agricultural: livestock provide food and other useful products; some insects pollinate crops.
  • Economic: fisheries, beekeeping and responsible livestock production support livelihoods.
  • Scientific and medical: selected animals help researchers study biological processes and develop treatments.
  • Environmental indicators: changes in animal populations can provide evidence of habitat damage or pollution.

Animals can also cause harm: some transmit disease, damage crops or act as parasites. Good management protects human health while conserving biodiversity.

6. Revision questions

  1. State four general characteristics of animals.
  2. What is the difference between autotrophic and heterotrophic nutrition?
  3. Distinguish radial symmetry from bilateral symmetry and give one example of each.
  4. Name the three germ layers of a triploblastic animal.
  5. What is a coelom?
  6. Give three characteristics of arthropods.
  7. Explain the difference between invertebrates and vertebrates.
  8. State two ecological benefits of animals.
  9. Are all animals actively mobile as adults? Explain with an example.
  10. Why is it inaccurate to treat all invertebrates as one phylum?

Answer key

  1. Any four: multicellular, eukaryotic, heterotrophic, lack cell walls, usually capable of movement at some life stage, respond to stimuli, and commonly reproduce sexually.
  2. Autotrophs manufacture organic food from inorganic materials, often by photosynthesis. Heterotrophs obtain organic nutrients from other organisms or organic matter.
  3. Radial symmetry allows similar halves through multiple planes around an axis, as in a jellyfish. Bilateral symmetry has one main plane dividing right and left halves, as in an earthworm.
  4. Ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm.
  5. A fluid-filled body cavity between the body wall and digestive tract that is completely lined by mesoderm-derived tissue.
  6. Jointed appendages, segmented body, and a chitinous external skeleton.
  7. Invertebrates lack a vertebral column; vertebrates possess one.
  8. Examples include pollination, seed dispersal, nutrient cycling and maintaining food webs.
  9. No. Adult sponges are generally sessile, although many animals move during at least one life stage.
  10. Invertebrates include many different phyla; the term describes the absence of a vertebral column rather than one single phylum.
Study tip: Practise comparing animal groups by symmetry, tissue organization, body cavity, segmentation and key structures. Use examples to support each comparison.
