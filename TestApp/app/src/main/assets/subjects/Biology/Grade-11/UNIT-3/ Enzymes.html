3.1 What are enzymes?

💭 Self‑questioning: “What do I know about enzymes, their properties and functions, and what do I want to learn from this section?”

Enzymes are protein molecules that act as biological catalysts (biocatalysts) and accelerate rate of chemical reactions by lowering activation energy. Activation energy is the minimum energy required for reactant to be converted to products. All enzymes are proteins made up of chains of amino acids linked by peptide bonds.

Metabolism (catabolism + anabolism) requires enzymes to catalyze reactions fast enough to sustain life. Enzymes act upon substrates, convert them into products, and remain unchanged.

3.2 Properties and functions of enzymes

3.2.1 General properties

A. Physical properties

  • Denaturation: loss of shape by heat (>40°C), extreme pH, heavy metals.
  • Solubility: dissolved in water, salt, dilute glycerol; alcohol causes denaturation.
  • Colloids: large size, no dialysis through semipermeable membrane.
  • Biocatalyst: small amount converts large quantity, unchanged after reaction.
  • Precipitation: separated using solvents.
  • Molecular weight: high, due to polypeptide chains.
  • Enzyme activity: depends on temp, pH, [enzyme], [substrate].

B. Chemical properties

  • Heat & pH sensitivity: active at optimum.
  • Regulation: controlled by activators/inhibitors.
  • Catalysis: high efficiency (100–10,000 substrates per second).
  • Reversibility: catalyze both forward/backward (anabolic & catabolic).
  • Specificity: bond, group, substrate, optical, co‑factor.

3.2.2 Function of enzymes

Enzymes speed up chemical reactions (digestion, respiration, nerve function). Turnover number = substrates converted per second per enzyme at saturation. Examples: sucrase, lactase, carbohydrase, lipase, protease.

3.3 Protein structures

💭 Self‑question: “What do I know about protein structure and what do I want to learn?”
  • Primary: amino acid sequence (peptide bonds).
  • Secondary: α‑helix (right‑handed coil, H‑bonds) and β‑pleated sheet (inter‑strand H‑bonds).
  • Tertiary: 3D shape from side‑chain interactions (disulfide, H‑bonds, etc.).
  • Quaternary: multiple polypeptide subunits (e.g., hemoglobin).

3.5 Enzyme regulation

Allosteric enzymes: have additional binding sites (regulatory site) for effectors (activators/inhibitors) causing conformational change.
Covalent modification: phosphorylation (add phosphate) activates/deactivates.
Inhibition: competitive (active site blocked), non‑competitive (binds elsewhere), uncompetitive (binds ES complex).

3.6 Types of enzymes

3.6.1 Structural classification

Simple protein (active) / Conjugated protein (holoenzyme = apoenzyme + cofactor). Cofactor: prosthetic group (firm) or coenzyme (loose, vitamin B).

3.6.2 Basic classification (six classes)

  • Oxidoreductases: electron transfer.
  • Transferases: transfer functional groups.
  • Hydrolases: hydrolysis.
  • Lyases: cleave bonds without hydrolysis/oxidation.
  • Isomerases: isomer conversion.
  • Ligases: join molecules with ATP.

3.7 Factors affecting enzyme action

  • Temperature: optimum ~37°C (human); high temp denatures.
  • pH: optimum varies (e.g., pepsin pH 2, trypsin pH 8).
  • Substrate concentration: increases rate until saturation.
  • Radiation, water, end‑product inhibition (feedback).

3.8 Enzyme kinetics

Michaelis‑Menten equation: \( V_1 = \frac{V_{max}[S]}{K_M + [S]} \). \(K_M\) = [S] at half Vmax. Describes reaction rate vs substrate.

3.9 Application of enzymes in industries

Used in food, feed, textile, paper, leather, detergents, pharmaceuticals. Examples: protease in detergents, cellulase in biofuel, rennin in cheese.

📋 Unit three summary

Enzymes are protein biocatalysts lowering activation energy. Activity depends on temp, pH, [substrate], inhibitors/activators. Cofactors (coenzymes) help. Models: lock‑key, induced fit.
