Unit 1: Introduction to Biology

Subject: Biology | Grade: 9 | Curriculum: Ethiopia, new curriculum

Learning purpose: Understand how biology helps us investigate living things, solve problems, and make evidence-based decisions.

1. Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Define biology and identify its major branches.
  • Explain the importance of biology in health, agriculture, industry, and environmental protection.
  • Describe the stages of a scientific investigation.
  • Identify common laboratory and field tools used by biologists.
  • Name the main parts of a light microscope and explain safe focusing procedures.
  • Apply basic laboratory safety rules.

2. What is biology?

Biology is the scientific study of life and living organisms. It investigates how organisms are structured, how they function, how they reproduce, how they interact with one another, and how they relate to their environment.

Biology studies organisms ranging from microscopic bacteria to large plants and animals. It also investigates processes such as growth, nutrition, respiration, inheritance, and adaptation.

Some branches of biology

  • Botany: the study of plants.
  • Zoology: the study of animals.
  • Microbiology: the study of microscopic organisms and biological agents.
  • Ecology: the study of relationships between organisms and their environment.
  • Genetics: the study of heredity and variation.
  • Anatomy: the study of organism structures.
  • Physiology: the study of how body parts and organisms function.

3. Why do we study biology?

  • Health: understanding the human body, disease transmission, prevention, and healthy living.
  • Agriculture: improving crop production, protecting livestock, and managing pests responsibly.
  • Environment: conserving biodiversity, protecting habitats, and understanding pollution.
  • Food: learning about nutrition, food safety, and microorganisms involved in food production.
  • Biotechnology: applying living systems and biological knowledge to useful products and processes.
  • Research: developing evidence-based solutions to local and global problems.

For example, a farmer may compare crop growth under different watering conditions to determine which practice conserves water while supporting healthy growth.

4. The scientific method

A scientific investigation is a systematic way of answering a question using observations, measurements, and evidence. Scientists may repeat steps or revise an explanation when new evidence appears.

  1. Observation: notice a pattern or problem.
  2. Question: state what you want to investigate.
  3. Background research: learn what is already known.
  4. Hypothesis: propose a testable explanation or prediction.
  5. Plan and experiment: choose a fair method and record the procedure.
  6. Collect data: record observations and measurements accurately.
  7. Analyze: organize results in tables or graphs and look for patterns.
  8. Conclusion: explain whether the evidence supports the hypothesis.
  9. Communicate: report the method, results, limitations, and conclusion.
Example: Question: Does the amount of water affect bean-seedling growth? Hypothesis: Seedlings receiving a suitable amount of water will grow better than seedlings receiving too little water. Keep seed type, soil, container, light, and observation period as similar as possible. Measure growth and record results. Do not claim a conclusion before collecting evidence.

Variables in an investigation

  • Independent variable: the factor deliberately changed.
  • Dependent variable: the outcome measured.
  • Controlled variables: factors kept the same to make the comparison fair.

Scientific results should be based on evidence, not personal opinion. One investigation may have limitations, so repeating it and comparing results can improve confidence.

6. The light microscope

A light microscope uses visible light and lenses to make small objects appear larger. Magnification helps us see details that are not easily visible to the unaided eye.

Main parts and functions

  • Eyepiece: lens viewed through by the observer.
  • Objective lenses: provide different magnification levels.
  • Revolving nosepiece: holds and changes objective lenses.
  • Stage: supports the slide.
  • Stage clips or slide holder: secures the slide.
  • Light source or mirror: directs light through the specimen.
  • Diaphragm: controls the amount of light reaching the specimen.
  • Coarse adjustment knob: moves the stage or body tube substantially for initial focusing, as appropriate to the microscope.
  • Fine adjustment knob: makes small focusing changes.
  • Arm: supports the upper part and helps when carrying the microscope.
  • Base: supports the microscope.

Magnification

Total magnification = eyepiece magnification × objective magnification.

Example: a 10× eyepiece and a 4× objective give a total magnification of 40×.

Safe focusing procedure

  1. Carry the microscope upright using two hands: one on the arm and one under the base.
  2. Place it on a stable, clean surface.
  3. Begin with the lowest-power objective lens.
  4. Place the prepared slide on the stage and secure it.
  5. Adjust the light so the field of view is clear.
  6. Look from the side while bringing the objective and slide into a safe starting position.
  7. Look through the eyepiece and focus slowly, following the microscope's instructions.
  8. Use fine adjustment for a sharp image. At high power, avoid coarse adjustment unless the microscope's instructions specifically allow it.
  9. When finished, remove the slide, return to low power, and clean and store the microscope as instructed.

Important: Never allow an objective lens to strike the slide. Do not touch lenses with fingers; use lens paper when instructed.

7. General laboratory safety

  • Follow the teacher's instructions and read labels before using materials.
  • Wear required protective equipment, such as goggles or a laboratory coat.
  • Never taste laboratory materials or pipette by mouth.
  • Do not eat or drink in the laboratory.
  • Never mix chemicals or handle unknown specimens without permission.
  • Handle glassware, blades, flames, and electrical equipment only as instructed.
  • Report spills, breakages, injuries, or unsafe conditions immediately.
  • Keep the work area tidy and wash hands after practical work.
  • Dispose of waste according to the teacher's directions.
  • Do not collect, culture, or handle unknown microorganisms at home.

8. Practical activities

Activity A: Observation and evidence

Observe a safe, familiar object such as a leaf. Record its visible features without guessing about features you cannot see. Separate observations (what you directly notice) from explanations (what you think those observations mean).

Record: date, object, conditions, observations, and one question for further investigation.

Activity B: Planning a fair investigation

Plan a classroom investigation comparing bean-seedling growth under two teacher-approved watering conditions. Identify the independent variable, dependent variable, and at least three controlled variables. Use the same observation period and record measurements in a table. Follow the teacher's directions for seed handling and disposal.

Activity C: Microscope observation

Under teacher supervision, observe a prepared, safe slide. Begin at low power, focus carefully, and draw what you actually see. Label visible features only; do not copy a diagram as though it were your observation.

10. Answer key and explanations

  1. B. Biology investigates living organisms and life processes.
  2. C. Botany is the study of plants.
  3. B, 100×. Total magnification = 10 × 10.
  4. B. Low power gives a wider field of view and makes initial focusing easier.
  5. C. The independent variable is deliberately changed.
  6. C. Spills should be reported promptly so they can be handled safely.
  7. Biology is the scientific study of life and living organisms. Examples of branches include botany, zoology, ecology, genetics, and microbiology.
  8. Examples include disease prevention, improved crop production, livestock health, food safety, and conservation of biodiversity.
  9. A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation or prediction. A conclusion interprets the evidence collected during the investigation.
  10. The independent variable is the amount of water; the dependent variable is plant height.
  11. Examples: the objective lenses magnify the specimen; the stage supports the slide; the diaphragm controls light; the fine adjustment sharpens focus.
  12. Any four appropriate rules, such as follow instructions, wear required protection, never taste materials, report spills, keep the area tidy, and wash hands.
  13. Sunlight is an uncontrolled variable. Give both plants the same light and other conditions, change only the fertilizer treatment, use suitable replicates, and measure growth consistently.
  14. Accurate records reduce bias, allow other people to evaluate the evidence, and help determine whether the hypothesis is supported.

11. Lesson summary

  • Biology is the scientific study of life.
  • Biological knowledge supports health, agriculture, research, and environmental conservation.
  • Scientific investigations use questions, testable hypotheses, fair methods, observations, data analysis, and evidence-based conclusions.
  • Biologists use specialized tools, including microscopes, to observe and measure.
  • Careful microscope handling and laboratory safety protect people, equipment, and specimens.
