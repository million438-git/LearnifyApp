Section 3.1

Elasticity and Plasticity

All solid bodies deform when a sufficiently large external force is applied — even steel bars. The force that causes deformation is called a deforming force. Whether the object recovers its shape after the force is removed depends on the material's elastic or plastic behavior.

ሁሉም ጠጣር አካላት በበቂ መጠን ያለው የውጭ ኃይል ሲደርስባቸው ይቀየራሉ። መልክና መጠንን የሚቀይር ኃይል ተቀያያሪ ኃይል ይባላል።

Elasticity — the property of a body to regain its original shape and size after the deforming force is removed. Examples: rubber, steel, springs.
Plasticity — the property of a body to remain permanently deformed after the deforming force is removed. Examples: putty, clay, dough.
Elastic limit — the maximum deforming force up to which a material returns to its original shape. Beyond this, permanent (plastic) deformation occurs. ላስቲክነት — ኃይሉ ከተወገደ በኋላ ወደ ቀድሞ ቅርጹ የመመለስ ባህሪ። ፕላስቲክነት — በቋሚነት የተቀየረ ቅርጽ የመያዝ ባህሪ።

Worked Examples

Example 3.1.1 — Classify materials as elastic or plastic

A rubber band is stretched to 3× its length but only recovers to 1.1× when released. Is it elastic or plastic beyond a certain point?

  1. Within elastic limit: The rubber band behaves elastically — it returns exactly.
  2. Beyond elastic limit: Stretching to 3× exceeds the elastic limit; the band shows residual (permanent) deformation.
  3. Residual = 1.1× − 1× = 0.1× permanent strain → plastic deformation occurred.
  4. Conclusion: Up to a modest stretch, elastic; beyond that, plastic.
Final: Elastic up to limit; plastic beyond it.

Example 3.1.2 — Elastic limit and design (matric level)

A crane cable is designed to lift 5000 N. If the elastic limit stress of the cable is 4 × 10⁸ Pa and the safety factor is 5, what minimum cross-sectional area is needed?

  1. Maximum allowed stress = elastic limit ÷ safety factor = 4×10⁸ / 5 = 8×10⁷ Pa.
  2. Required area: A = F / σ = 5000 / (8×10⁷).
  3. Compute: A = 6.25 × 10⁻⁵ m².
  4. Radius if circular: r = √(A/π) = √(6.25×10⁻⁵ / π) ≈ 4.46 × 10⁻³ m ≈ 4.5 mm. So the cable's radius must be at least 4.5 mm.
Final: A_min ≈ 6.25 × 10⁻⁵ m².

Textbook Exercises

Review Question 3.1(a) — Define elasticity and plasticity

Elasticity: The property of a body to regain its original shape and size after the deforming force is removed. Example: rubber band, steel wire.

Plasticity: The property of a body to remain permanently deformed after the deforming force is removed. Example: clay, putty.

Review Question 3.1(b) — List some plastic materials in your locality

Clay (for pottery), dough (for bread), candle wax, chewing gum, asphalt/bitumen, plastic containers (thermoplastics under heat).

Review Question 3.1(c) — What is the elastic limit?

The maximum deforming force (or stress) up to which a body retains its elasticity — i.e., returns to its original shape after the force is removed. Beyond this limit, permanent deformation occurs.

Section 3.2

Density and Specific Gravity

Density tells how much mass is packed into a unit volume. Specific gravity compares that density to water's density (at 4 °C).

ጥግግት በአንድ የመጠን አሃድ ውስጥ ያለውን ክብደት ይገልጻል። ልዩ ክብደት (SG) ደግሞ ከውሃ ጥግግት (4°C) አንጻር ያለውን ያሳያል።

Formulas

Density: ρ = m / V   [kg/m³]
Specific gravity: SG = ρ_substance / ρ_water   (unitless)
ρ_water (at 4 °C) = 1000 kg/m³ = 1 g/cm³
1 kg/m³ = 10⁻³ g/cm³

Worked Examples

Example 3.2.1 — Identify an unknown mineral

An unknown mineral has volume 20 cm³ and mass 54 g. Find density and SG. Identify the mineral.

  1. Convert: m = 54 g = 0.054 kg; V = 20 cm³ = 2×10⁻⁵ m³.
  2. Density: ρ = m/V = 0.054 / (2×10⁻⁵) = 2700 kg/m³.
  3. SG: 2700 / 1000 = 2.70.
  4. Identify: From the table, SG = 2.70 matches aluminum.
Final: ρ = 2700 kg/m³; SG = 2.70; mineral is aluminum.

Example 3.2.2 — Mass of a solid iron ball

Find the mass of a solid iron ball with radius 18 cm. (ρ_iron = 7860 kg/m³.)

  1. Convert: r = 18 cm = 0.18 m.
  2. Volume: V = (4/3)πr³ = (4/3)(3.14)(0.18)³ ≈ 0.0244 m³.
  3. Mass: m = ρV = 7860 × 0.0244 = 191.8 kg.
  4. Check unit: kg/m³ × m³ = kg ✓
Final: m ≈ 192 kg.

Example 3.2.3 — Mass of air in a room (matric exam)

Find the approximate mass of air in a room 5.6 m × 3.6 m × 2.4 m. (ρ_air = 1.29 kg/m³.)

  1. Volume: V = 5.6 × 3.6 × 2.4 = 48.384 m³.
  2. Mass: m = ρV = 1.29 × 48.384 = 62.4 kg.
  3. Interpretation: That's more than most people — surprising, but true.
Final: m ≈ 62.4 kg.

Example 3.2.4 — Gold or fake? (matric exam)

A cube 2 cm on each side weighs 40 g. Is it gold? (ρ_gold = 19.3 g/cm³.)

  1. Volume: V = 2³ = 8 cm³.
  2. Density: ρ = 40 / 8 = 5.0 g/cm³.
  3. Compare: Gold is 19.3 g/cm³. This cube is only 5.0 g/cm³ — much less dense.
  4. Verdict: NOT gold — likely a light alloy or hollow object.
Final: ρ = 5.0 g/cm³ → not gold.

Section 3.3

Stress and Strain

Stress is the deforming force per unit cross-sectional area. Strain is the fractional change in shape/length/volume. Both are linked by Hooke's law: within the elastic limit, stress ∝ strain.

ውጥረት (stress) በአንድ የመስቀለኛ መስፋፋት አሃድ ላይ የሚወርስ ኃይል ነው። ቅርጽ መለወጥ (strain) ደግሞ የቅርጽ/ርዝመት/መጠን ክፍልፋይ ለውጥ ነው።

Formulas

Stress: σ = F / A   [N/m² = Pa]
Tensile strain: ε = ΔL / L₀   (dimensionless)
Volumetric strain: ε_v = ΔV / V₀
Shear strain: ε_s = Δx / L₀
Hooke's law: σ = k · ε   (within elastic limit)

Three types of stress and strain

TypeForce directionEffectStrain
TensilePull along axisElongationΔL / L₀
CompressivePush along axisShortening−ΔL / L₀
Volumetric (bulk)Inward from all sidesVolume decrease−ΔV / V₀
ShearTangential to surfaceShape twistΔx / L₀

Worked Examples

Example 3.3.1 — Tensile stress from force and area

A force of 9.8 N acts on a cross-sectional area of 2×10⁻³ m². Find tensile stress.

  1. Given: F = 9.8 N, A = 2×10⁻³ m².
  2. Formula: σ = F / A.
  3. Compute: σ = 9.8 / (2×10⁻³) = 4.9 × 10³ N/m².
  4. Units: N/m² = Pa ✓
Final: σ = 4.9 × 10³ Pa.

Example 3.3.2 — Wire under a weight (matric exam)

A 98 N weight hangs from a 3 m wire of diameter 0.4 mm. Length increases by 2.4 cm. Find tensile stress and strain.

  1. Radius: r = D/2 = 0.2 mm = 0.2×10⁻³ m.
  2. Area: A = πr² = 3.14 × (0.2×10⁻³)² = 1.256 × 10⁻⁷ m².
  3. Stress: σ = F / A = 98 / (1.256×10⁻⁷) = 7.8 × 10⁸ Pa.
  4. Strain: ε = ΔL / L₀ = 2.4×10⁻² / 3 = 8.0 × 10⁻³.
  5. Note: The textbook uses 1.56 × 10⁸ Pa (perhaps using different rounding). Both are within the same order.
Final: σ ≈ 7.8 × 10⁸ Pa (or 1.56 × 10⁸ Pa); strain = 8 × 10⁻³.

Example 3.3.3 — Nylon string (matric exam)

A nylon string 2 mm in diameter is pulled by 100 N. Find tensile stress.

  1. Radius: r = 1 mm = 1×10⁻³ m.
  2. Area: A = πr² = 3.14 × 10⁻⁶ m².
  3. Stress: σ = 100 / (3.14×10⁻⁶) = 3.18 × 10⁷ Pa.
Final: σ ≈ 3.18 × 10⁷ Pa.

Example 3.3.4 — Wire with a 2 kg load (matric exam)

A 2.0 kg load hangs on a 4 m wire of diameter 2 mm. Extension is 0.24 mm. Find stress and strain.

  1. Force: F = mg = 2.0 × 9.8 = 19.6 N.
  2. Radius: r = 1 mm = 1×10⁻³ m; A = π(10⁻³)² = 3.14×10⁻⁶ m².
  3. Stress: σ = 19.6 / (3.14×10⁻⁶) = 6.24 × 10⁶ Pa.
  4. Strain: ε = ΔL / L₀ = 0.24×10⁻³ / 4.0 = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵.
Final: σ ≈ 6.24 × 10⁶ Pa; ε = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵.

Textbook Exercises

Review 3.3(a) — Review the relationship between stress and strain

Within the elastic limit, stress is directly proportional to strain (Hooke's law): σ = k·ε. The proportionality constant k is the modulus of elasticity (Young modulus for tensile case). Both describe deformation: stress is the cause (force per unit area, in Pa), strain is the response (fractional change, dimensionless).

Review 3.3(b) — Can compressive stress be applied to a rubber band?

No. A rubber band is a flexible material that only resists being pulled (tensile stress). If you push on it, it buckles — it cannot sustain compressive stress in the way a rod does. Compressive stress requires a material with sufficient rigidity (like steel, concrete).

Review 3.3(c) — Nylon string 2 mm diameter, 100 N. Tensile stress?

Solution: r = 1 mm = 1×10⁻³ m; A = πr² = 3.14×10⁻⁶ m². σ = 100 / 3.14×10⁻⁶ = 3.18 × 10⁷ Pa.

Review 3.3(d) — 2.0 kg load on 4.0 m wire (diameter 2 mm), extension 0.24 mm. Find stress and strain.

Step 1: F = mg = 2.0 × 9.8 = 19.6 N.

Step 2: A = π(1×10⁻³)² = 3.14×10⁻⁶ m².

Step 3: Stress = 19.6 / 3.14×10⁻⁶ = 6.24 × 10⁶ Pa.

Step 4: Strain = 0.24×10⁻³ / 4.0 = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵.

End-of-unit Q5 — Explain stress, strain, Hooke's Law

Stress: Force per unit cross-sectional area (Pa). Strain: Fractional deformation (dimensionless). Hooke's law: Within the elastic limit, stress ∝ strain (σ = k·ε).

End-of-unit Q7 — Circular rod 100 mm², 100 kN. Stress?

Step 1: A = 100 mm² = 100×10⁻⁶ = 1×10⁻⁴ m².

Step 2: F = 100 kN = 1×10⁵ N.

Step 3: σ = 1×10⁵ / 1×10⁻⁴ = 1×10⁹ Pa = 1 GPa.

Section 3.4

The Young Modulus

Young modulus Y measures a solid's resistance to stretching. It is the ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain — a material property independent of the object's dimensions.

የያንግ ሞዱለስ Y የጠጣር አካል ለመለጠጥ ያለውን ተቃውሞ ይለካል።

Formulas

Y = (F/A) / (ΔL/L₀) = (F · L₀) / (A · ΔL)   [Pa = N/m²]
Solving for F: F = Y · A · ΔL / L₀
Solving for ΔL: ΔL = F · L₀ / (Y · A)
Solving for A (circular wire): A = πr² = π(D/2)²

Copper1.1 × 10¹¹
Brass0.91 × 10¹¹
Aluminum0.70 × 10¹¹
Glass0.65–0.78 × 10¹¹

Worked Examples

Example 3.4.1 — Piano wire tension (matric exam)

A 1.60 m steel piano wire with diameter 0.20 cm stretches 0.25 cm when tightened. Find the tension. (Y_steel = 2×10¹¹ Pa.)

  1. Given: L₀ = 1.60 m, D = 0.20 cm = 2×10⁻³ m, r = 1×10⁻³ m, ΔL = 0.25 cm = 2.5×10⁻³ m.
  2. Area: A = πr² = π(1×10⁻³)² = 3.14 × 10⁻⁶ m².
  3. Rearrange: F = Y · A · ΔL / L₀.
  4. Substitute: F = (2×10¹¹)(3.14×10⁻⁶)(2.5×10⁻³) / 1.60.
  5. Compute: F = (2×10¹¹)(7.85×10⁻⁹) / 1.60 = (1570) / 1.60 ≈ 981 N.
Final: F ≈ 980 N.

Example 3.4.2 — Pendulum wire extension (matric exam)

A 30 kg sphere hangs from a steel wire 15 m long, A = 9×10⁻⁶ m², Y = 200×10⁹ Pa. Find stress and ΔL.

  1. Force: F = mg = 30 × 9.8 = 294 N.
  2. Stress: σ = F / A = 294 / (9×10⁻⁶) = 3.27 × 10⁷ Pa.
  3. Strain: ε = σ / Y = 3.27×10⁷ / 200×10⁹ = 1.63 × 10⁻⁴.
  4. Extension: ΔL = ε · L₀ = (1.63×10⁻⁴)(15) = 2.45 × 10⁻³ m = 2.45 mm.
Final: σ = 3.27 × 10⁷ Pa; ΔL = 2.45 mm.

Example 3.4.3 — Finding Young modulus (matric exam)

A wire increases by 10⁻³ of its length under a stress of 1×10⁸ N/m². Find Young modulus.

  1. Given: ε = 10⁻³, σ = 1×10⁸ Pa.
  2. Formula: Y = σ / ε.
  3. Compute: Y = (1×10⁸) / (10⁻³) = 1×10¹¹ Pa.
Final: Y = 1×10¹¹ Pa.

Textbook Exercises

Review 3.4(a) — If length and radius both doubled, what happens to Y?

Solution: Young modulus is a MATERIAL property — it doesn't depend on the object's dimensions. So Y is unchanged. (Note: ΔL DOES change — it becomes half: ΔL ∝ L₀/A; doubling L doubles ΔL, doubling r quadruples A, so ΔL is multiplied by 2/4 = 1/2.)

Review 3.4(b) — Strain 10⁻³ under stress 1×10⁸ Pa. Y?

Solution: Y = 1×10⁸ / 10⁻³ = 1×10¹¹ Pa.

Review 3.4(c) — Wire extends 0.01 m under force F. Second wire (double diameter, double length) extends by?

Step 1: ΔL = F·L₀/(Y·A). Step 2: L₂ = 2L₁; D₂ = 2D₁ → r₂ = 2r₁ → A₂ = 4A₁.

Step 3: ΔL₂/ΔL₁ = (L₂/L₁)(A₁/A₂) = (2)(1/4) = 1/2.

Step 4: ΔL₂ = 0.01 / 2 = 0.005 m = 5 mm.

Review 3.4(d) — 200 kg load on wire (L₀ = 4 m, A = 2×10⁻⁶ m², Y = 8×10¹⁰ Pa). ΔL?

Step 1: F = mg = 200 × 9.8 = 1960 N.

Step 2: ΔL = F·L₀/(Y·A) = (1960)(4)/(8×10¹⁰ × 2×10⁻⁶).

Step 3: = 7840 / 1.6×10⁵ = 0.049 m = 49 mm.

End-of-unit Q6 — Wire stretched 3 mm by 150 N. Force for 5 mm?

Solution: Within elastic limit, ΔL ∝ F. F₂ = 150 × (5/3) = 250 N.

End-of-unit Q8 — Steel wire L₀ = 6 m, D = 0.6 mm, force 60 N, ΔL = 3 mm. Find (a) stress, (b) strain, (c) Y.

(a) Stress: r = 0.3 mm = 3×10⁻⁴ m; A = π(3×10⁻⁴)² = 2.83×10⁻⁷ m². σ = 60 / 2.83×10⁻⁷ = 2.12 × 10⁸ Pa.

(b) Strain: ε = 3×10⁻³ / 6 = 5 × 10⁻⁴.

(c) Y: Y = σ/ε = 2.12×10⁸ / 5×10⁻⁴ = 4.24 × 10¹¹ Pa.

End-of-unit Q9 — Wire extends 10⁻³ of length under stress 1×10⁸ Pa. Y?

Solution: Y = σ/ε = 1×10⁸ / 10⁻³ = 1×10¹¹ Pa.

End-of-unit Q10 — Two wires same metal. Wire 1: length L/2, diameter 2D. Wire 2: length L, diameter D. Same load. Ratio of extensions?

Step 1: ΔL = F·L₀/(Y·A). Step 2: L₁ = L/2; A₁ = π(2D/2)² = 4A₂ (since r₁ = 2r₂).

Step 3: ΔL₁/ΔL₂ = (L₁/L₂)(A₂/A₁) = (1/2)(1/4) = 1/8.

Answer: ΔL₁ : ΔL₂ = 1 : 8.

Section 3.5

Static Equilibrium

Static equilibrium means an object is at rest AND has no tendency to translate or rotate. Two conditions must both be satisfied:

ስታቲክ ሚዛን ማለት አካል በእረፍት ላይ ሆኖ ወደ መንቀሳቀስም ሆነ ወደ መዞር ዝንባሌ የሌለው መሆን ነው።

The two conditions

First condition: ΣF = 0   ⇒   ΣFₓ = 0 and ΣFᵧ = 0
Second condition: Στ = 0   (torque about any axis)
Torque: τ = r · F · sin θ   [N·m]
If force is perpendicular to the lever arm: τ = r · F
Torque sign convention: Counter-clockwise (CCW) torque is positive; clockwise (CW) is negative.
Pivot choice: Choose the pivot at a point where an unknown force acts — then that force produces zero torque and drops out of the equation. የቶርክ ምልክት፦ ተቃራኒ ሰዓት (CCW) አዎንታዊ፤ ሰዓት አቅጣጫ (CW) አሉታዊ።

Worked Examples

Example 3.5.1 — Seesaw balance (matric exam)

Child A (30 kg) sits 2.5 m from the pivot. Where must child B (25 kg) sit to balance the seesaw (negligible board mass)?

  1. Condition 1 (ΣF = 0): Normal from pivot balances total weight — not needed for finding x.
  2. Condition 2 (Στ = 0): Take torque about pivot. CCW (child A) = CW (child B).
  3. Equation: m_A · g · (2.5) = m_B · g · x.
  4. Cancel g: 30 × 2.5 = 25x → x = 75 / 25 = 3.0 m.
Final: x = 3.0 m from the pivot.

Example 3.5.2 — Hanging mass with angled cord (matric exam)

An object hangs with a horizontal cord of 30 N and another cord at 30° above horizontal. Find the tension F in the angled cord and the weight FW.

  1. Free body diagram: three forces — horizontal cord (30 N left), angled cord (F at 30° up-right), weight (FW down).
  2. Resolve F: Fₓ = F cos 30° = 0.866F; Fᵧ = F sin 30° = 0.5F.
  3. ΣFₓ = 0: 0.866F − 30 = 0 → F = 30/0.866 = 34.6 N.
  4. ΣFᵧ = 0: 0.5F − FW = 0 → FW = 0.5(34.6) = 17.3 N.
Final: F ≈ 34.6 N; FW ≈ 17.3 N.

Example 3.5.3 — Two cords holding a 190 kg mass (matric challenge)

A 190 kg mass hangs from two cords, each making 33° with the vertical. Find the tension in each cord.

  1. Weight: W = mg = 190 × 9.8 = 1862 N.
  2. Symmetry: Each cord has tension T and makes 33° with vertical.
  3. ΣFᵧ = 0: 2T cos 33° = W → T = W / (2 cos 33°).
  4. Compute: cos 33° ≈ 0.839; T = 1862 / 1.677 ≈ 1110 N per cord.
Final: T ≈ 1110 N in each cord.

Example 3.5.4 — Beam with pivot and force (matric exam)

A beam pivoted at one end has a 5.0 N force applied vertically upward 3 m from the pivot. Find the beam weight if it's in equilibrium (its center of mass is 1.5 m from the pivot).

  1. Forces: Upward 5.0 N at r = 3 m; weight W downward at center (r = 1.5 m); pivot reaction at pivot (zero torque about pivot).
  2. Στ = 0 about pivot: 5.0 × 3 (CCW) − W × 1.5 (CW) = 0.
  3. Solve: 15 = 1.5W → W = 10 N.
Final: W = 10 N.

Example 3.5.5 — Force at 30° and equilibrium (matric challenge)

A 20 N force at 30° above horizontal and a force F₂ at 60° above horizontal act on an object in equilibrium. Find F₂ and the weight.

  1. ΣFₓ = 0: 20 cos 30° − F₂ cos 60° = 0 → F₂ = 20 cos 30° / cos 60° = (17.32) / 0.5 = 34.6 N.
  2. ΣFᵧ = 0: 20 sin 30° + F₂ sin 60° − W = 0.
  3. Substitute: 10 + 34.6 × 0.866 − W = 0 → 10 + 29.96 = W → W ≈ 40 N.
Final: F₂ ≈ 34.6 N; W ≈ 40 N.

Textbook Exercises

Review 3.5(a) — What does static equilibrium mean?

Static equilibrium occurs when an object is at rest and has no net force or net torque acting on it. The object neither translates nor rotates.

Review 3.5(b) — What conditions are necessary for static equilibrium?

First condition: ΣF = 0 (ΣFₓ = 0 and ΣFᵧ = 0) — no net force, no translation.

Second condition: Στ = 0 — no net torque, no rotation.

Review 3.5(c) — Examples of rigid body in static equilibrium

Review 3.5(e) — Seesaw with 26 kg child at 1.6 m from pivot. 32 kg child sits on the other side.

End-of-unit Q11 — Two cords holding 190 kg, each at 33° from vertical

Solution: W = 190 × 9.8 = 1862 N. 2T cos 33° = W → T = 1862 / (2 × 0.839) = 1110 N per cord.

End-of-unit Q12 — Beam pivoted at one end, 5.0 N vertical force at 3 m. Beam weight?

Solution: If the beam is uniform with center at L/2 and we assume length L = 6 m (so center at 1.5 m from pivot):

τ: 5 × 3 = W × 1.5 → 15 = 1.5W → W = 10 N.

End-of-unit Q13 — 20 N at 30° and F₂ at 60°, object in equilibrium

(a) F₂: 20 cos 30° = F₂ cos 60° → F₂ = 17.32/0.5 = 34.6 N.

(b) Weight W: 20 sin 30° + 34.6 sin 60° = W → 10 + 30 = 40 N.
