🌊 Grade 12 Physics – Chapter 3

Fluid mechanics · full deep‑dive notes + 60 ultimate questions

3.1 What exactly is a fluid?

Fluids are substances that can flow and take the shape of their container. Both liquids and gases are fluids.

  • Liquids have a definite volume but no fixed shape.
  • Gases have neither fixed volume nor fixed shape – they expand to fill the container.
💧 WOW: Even sand can behave like a fluid if shaken (it flows), but technically it’s a granular material. Real fluids include water, air, oil, and even molten lava.
📘 REMEMBER: The word “fluid” comes from the Latin fluere (to flow). In physics, fluids are studied using fluid mechanics.

3.2 Density – how packed is the matter?

Density tells us how much mass is squeezed into a given volume. In words: density = mass ÷ volume.

  • High density → heavy for its size (e.g., iron).
  • Low density → light for its size (e.g., wood, air).

Relative density compares a substance’s density to that of water. If relative density less than 1, it floats on water.

🏊 WOW: A huge ship made of steel floats because its average density (including the air inside) is less than water’s.
📘 REMEMBER: Density is why ice floats – ice has lower density than liquid water.

3.3 Pressure in fluids

Pressure = force ÷ area over which the force acts. Fluids exert pressure on every surface they touch.

Key facts about fluid pressure

  • Pressure increases with depth (because more fluid weight above).
  • At any given depth, pressure acts equally in all directions.
  • Pressure depends only on depth and fluid density – not on the shape of the container.

Example: Dams are built thicker at the bottom because water pressure is greatest there.

🌊 WOW: The pressure 10 m underwater is about twice the normal atmospheric pressure – that’s why your ears hurt when you dive deep.
📘 REMEMBER: Atmospheric pressure is the weight of air above us – about 10 N on every square centimetre at sea level.

3.4 Pascal’s principle – force multiplication

Pascal’s principle: Any change in pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of the fluid and to the walls of the container.

Real‑world hydraulic systems

  • Hydraulic lift – a small force on a small piston lifts a heavy load on a larger piston.
  • Hydraulic brakes – foot pressure on brake fluid forces brake pads against wheels.
  • Hydraulic press – used to shape metals.
🏗️ WOW: A car mechanic can lift a car with just one hand using a hydraulic jack – because pressure is the same everywhere, a small piston moves far and a large piston moves little but with huge force.
📘 REMEMBER: The ratio of forces equals the ratio of piston areas. Large area → large force.

3.5 Archimedes’ principle – the buoyancy secret

Archimedes’ principle: A body partially or fully immersed in a fluid experiences an upward force (buoyant force) equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces.

  • If buoyant force > object’s weight → object rises (floats).
  • If buoyant force < object’s weight → object sinks.

Why do ships float?

A ship is hollow and contains air, so its average density is less than water. It displaces a huge volume of water, creating a huge buoyant force.

🛳️ WOW: Legend says Archimedes discovered this principle while taking a bath – he ran naked through the streets shouting “Eureka!” (I found it!).
📘 REMEMBER: Buoyant force does not depend on the object’s weight, only on the weight of displaced fluid.

3.6 Floating, sinking & apparent weight

  • An object floats if its density is less than the fluid’s density.
  • It sinks if its density is greater.
  • If densities are equal, it can stay at any depth (neutral buoyancy).

Apparent weight = true weight – buoyant force. That’s why objects feel lighter underwater.

🎈 WOW: A hot‑air balloon floats because hot air is less dense than cool air – buoyancy in air works the same way as in water.

3.7 Fluid flow – smooth vs wild

Fluids can flow in two main ways:

  • Steady (laminar) flow – layers slide smoothly; no mixing.
  • Turbulent flow – chaotic, with eddies and swirls.

Whether flow is laminar or turbulent depends on speed, viscosity, and pipe shape.

🌪️ WOW: Blood flow in your arteries is normally laminar, but if it becomes turbulent, doctors can hear it with a stethoscope – it’s called a bruit.

3.8 The continuity principle – mass conservation

For an incompressible fluid, the product of cross‑sectional area and speed is constant along a pipe. In words:

When fluid flows from a wide pipe to a narrow one, its speed increases.

  • Example: river narrows → water flows faster.
  • Example: putting a thumb over a garden hose makes water shoot out faster.
📘 REMEMBER: Area × speed = constant (for same flow rate).

3.9 Bernoulli’s principle – speed and pressure

Bernoulli’s principle: In a flowing fluid, an increase in speed happens together with a decrease in pressure (or potential energy).

Amazing applications

  • Airplane wing: air moves faster over the curved top → lower pressure above → lift.
  • Atomizer / perfume sprayer: fast air above a tube lowers pressure, liquid rises and is blown out.
  • Venturi meter: measures flow speed by pressure drop.
  • Curve ball (Magnus effect): spin causes pressure difference, ball curves.
✈️ WOW: Bernoulli’s principle also explains why roofs can blow off in a storm – fast wind above roof lowers pressure, so higher inside pressure lifts it.
📘 REMEMBER: Where speed is high, pressure is low – the principle behind many everyday things.

3.10 Viscosity – internal stickiness

Viscosity is a measure of a fluid’s resistance to flow. High viscosity fluids (like honey, oil) flow slowly; low viscosity (water, air) flow easily.

  • Temperature affects viscosity: liquids become less viscous when heated; gases become more viscous.
  • Viscosity causes drag force on objects moving through fluid.
🍯 WOW: Lava can have a viscosity thousands of times greater than water – that’s why it moves so slowly.

3.11 Surface tension – the skin of liquids

Surface tension makes the surface of a liquid behave like a stretched elastic membrane. It’s caused by cohesive forces between molecules.

  • Water droplets are spherical because surface tension minimizes surface area.
  • Some insects can walk on water.
  • Capillary action (rise of water in a thin tube) is due to surface tension and adhesion.
🐜 WOW: A water strider insect uses surface tension to stand on water – its legs are water‑repellent and create tiny dimples.

3.12 High‑pressure safety

  • Pipes, boilers, and hydraulic systems must be strong enough to withstand high pressure.
  • Pressure relief valves prevent explosions.
  • Regular inspection prevents leaks and bursts.
📘 REMEMBER: High‑pressure fluids store a lot of energy – a sudden leak can be deadly.

📋 60 challenging questions (conceptual & numerical in words)

All answers are in plain English – no symbols, just crystal‑clear physics.

1. What is a fluid? Give two examples.
A fluid is a substance that can flow and take the shape of its container. Examples: water, air.
2. Differentiate between a liquid and a gas.
Liquids have a fixed volume but no fixed shape; gases have neither fixed volume nor fixed shape.
3. Define density in your own words.
Density is how much mass is packed into a certain volume. It equals mass divided by volume.
4. Why does ice float on water?
Because ice has a lower density than liquid water – about 0.9 times that of water.
5. What is pressure? Write the simple relation in words.
Pressure is force acting on a unit area. Pressure = force divided by area.
6. How does pressure change with depth in a liquid?
Pressure increases with depth because the weight of liquid above increases.
7. Why are dams thicker at the bottom?
Because water pressure is greatest at the bottom, so the dam needs to be stronger there.
8. State Pascal’s principle.
Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally in all directions throughout the fluid.
9. Give two examples of hydraulic devices.
Hydraulic lift and hydraulic brakes.
10. What is upthrust or buoyant force?
The upward force exerted by a fluid on an object immersed in it.
11. State Archimedes’ principle.
When an object is partially or fully immersed in a fluid, it experiences an upward force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced.
12. A stone feels lighter in water. Why?
Because water exerts an upward buoyant force that partly cancels its weight.
13. What condition determines whether an object floats or sinks?
If the object’s density is less than the fluid’s density, it floats. If greater, it sinks.
14. A ship made of steel floats, but a small steel nail sinks. Explain.
The ship is hollow and contains air, so its average density is less than water. The nail is solid steel with density much higher than water.
15. Define relative density.
Relative density is the ratio of the density of a substance to the density of water. It has no units.
16. What is apparent weight?
Apparent weight is the weight of an object as measured when immersed in a fluid – equal to true weight minus buoyant force.
17. Why do hot‑air balloons float?
Heating the air inside makes it less dense than the surrounding cool air, so buoyant force lifts the balloon.
18. What is meant by laminar flow?
Laminar flow is smooth, orderly flow where fluid layers slide past each other without mixing.
19. What is turbulent flow?
Turbulent flow is chaotic, with eddies, vortices, and mixing.
20. State the principle of continuity in words.
For an incompressible fluid, the product of cross‑sectional area and speed remains constant along a pipe. So where area is smaller, speed is higher.
21. Why does water flow faster in a narrow pipe?
Because the same volume of water must pass every section per second – narrower area means higher speed (continuity).
22. State Bernoulli’s principle.
In a flowing fluid, an increase in speed occurs simultaneously with a decrease in pressure (or potential energy).
23. How does an airplane wing generate lift?
The wing is curved on top, so air moves faster over the top surface, creating lower pressure above than below – net upward force.
24. Explain how a perfume sprayer works using Bernoulli.
Squeezing the bulb pushes air rapidly over a vertical tube. The high‑speed air lowers pressure at the top of the tube, so perfume rises and is blown out as a fine mist.
25. What is a Venturi meter?
A device that measures flow speed by constricting a pipe – the pressure drop at the narrow section indicates speed.
26. Why does a curveball curve?
The ball spins, causing air on one side to move faster (lower pressure) and on the other side slower (higher pressure) – the pressure difference makes it curve.
27. Define viscosity.
Viscosity is the internal friction of a fluid that resists flow. High viscosity fluids flow slowly.
28. How does temperature affect viscosity of liquids?
For liquids, viscosity decreases when temperature increases (they flow more easily).
29. What is surface tension?
Surface tension is the tendency of liquid surfaces to shrink into the minimum surface area, like a stretched elastic film.
30. Give an example of surface tension in daily life.
Water droplets forming beads on a waxed car; insects walking on water.
31. Why does a needle (carefully placed) float on water even though steel is denser?
Surface tension of water supports the needle – the water surface acts like a thin skin.
32. What is capillary action?
The rise or fall of a liquid in a narrow tube due to surface tension and adhesion to the tube walls.
33. Why is it easier to swim in sea water than in river water?
Sea water is denser because of dissolved salt, so it provides greater buoyant force.
34. A balloon filled with helium rises in air. Explain.
Helium is less dense than air, so the buoyant force (weight of displaced air) is greater than the weight of the balloon.
35. Why do submarines have ballast tanks?
By filling tanks with water (increase weight) or blowing them with air (decrease weight), they can control buoyancy to dive or surface.
36. Explain why a person can lie on a bed of nails if the nails are many?
Pressure = force/area. Many nails spread the weight over a large total area, so pressure on each nail is small and doesn’t puncture skin.
37. Why do sharp knives cut better than blunt ones?
A sharp edge has a very small area, so for the same force, the pressure is huge – enabling cutting.
38. Why do we use a straw to drink?
When you suck, you reduce pressure inside the straw, and atmospheric pressure pushes the liquid up.
39. What is atmospheric pressure? Approximate value?
Atmospheric pressure is the weight of air column per unit area. At sea level, it is about 101,300 newtons per square metre (or 10 newtons per square cm).
40. Why do we use hydraulic brakes in vehicles?
They transmit pressure quickly and equally to all wheels, and provide force multiplication – a small pedal force produces large braking force.
41. A block of wood floats in water with half its volume submerged. What is its relative density?
Relative density = 0.5. Because the weight of displaced water equals the weight of the block; if half is submerged, the block’s density is half that of water.
42. A hydrometer measures what property?
The relative density (specific gravity) of a liquid by how deep it floats.
43. Why does water come out of a hose faster when you partially cover the opening?
Reducing the outlet area increases speed (continuity principle) for the same flow rate.
44. What is the principle behind a water pump?
A pump creates a region of low pressure, and atmospheric pressure pushes water up into the pipe.
45. Why are bicycle tyres inflated to a certain pressure?
Correct pressure provides a balance between comfort (low pressure) and low rolling resistance (high pressure). Too high can cause bursting; too low can damage tyre.
46. A dam has a depth of 20 m. Where is the pressure greatest?
At the bottom (20 m depth), because pressure increases with depth.
47. Why does an egg float in salt water but sink in fresh water?
Salt water is denser than fresh water. The egg’s density is between them – it is less dense than salt water but more dense than fresh water.
48. Explain why a barometer uses mercury rather than water.
Mercury is much denser than water, so a mercury column is only about 76 cm high. A water barometer would need over 10 m height.
49. What is gauge pressure?
Gauge pressure is the pressure above atmospheric pressure. Absolute pressure = atmospheric + gauge.
50. Why do ears pop when climbing a mountain?
Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude. The pressure inside the ear adjusts by pushing air out through the Eustachian tube, causing a pop.
51. A balloon filled with air at ground level shrinks when taken high up. Why?
External pressure decreases, so the air inside expands (balloon expands), not shrinks. Actually balloon expands at high altitude – but if it were a rigid container, pressure inside would be higher? Wait, think: if balloon is flexible, it expands until internal pressure equals external. At high altitude, external pressure lower, so balloon expands. The question might be misstated; but we answer correctly: a flexible balloon expands.
52. How does a siphon work?
A siphon uses atmospheric pressure and gravity. Liquid is pushed up by atmospheric pressure into a region of lower pressure created by the descending column, then gravity pulls it down.
53. Why is it dangerous to heat a sealed liquid container?
Heating increases pressure inside (liquid may vaporize, increasing pressure further). If pressure exceeds container strength, it can explode.
54. Why do deep‑sea divers need to exhale when ascending?
As they ascend, external pressure decreases, so air in lungs expands. If they hold breath, lungs could overinflate and rupture.
55. Explain why a stream of water from a tap narrows as it falls.
The water accelerates due to gravity, so speed increases. From continuity, if speed increases, cross‑sectional area must decrease to keep flow rate constant.
56. What is the Magnus effect?
The Magnus effect is the curving of a spinning ball due to pressure difference caused by speed difference on opposite sides – related to Bernoulli.
57. Why do racing cars have inverted wings?
Inverted wings (airfoils) generate downforce – high pressure above, low below – pressing car to track for better grip.
58. Why do we use soap to blow bubbles?
Soap reduces surface tension of water, making it easier to stretch the film and form bubbles that last longer.
59. Explain why a small needle can float on water, but if you add soap, it sinks.
Soap reduces surface tension, so the water surface can no longer support the needle.
60. (ultimate challenge) A piece of wood floats in a container of water. If the container is on a weighing scale and the wood is placed in it (floating), does the scale reading change compared to when the wood is outside? Explain.
Yes, the scale reading increases by exactly the weight of the wood. Even though the wood floats, it displaces water equal to its own weight, so the total weight on the scale = container + water + wood. So the reading is the same as if you put the wood in the container without water (just resting on bottom).
