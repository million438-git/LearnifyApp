Two‑dimensional motion, rotation, and gravitation (expanded notes + 30 challenges)

2.1 Motion in a plane

When an object moves in both horizontal and vertical directions at the same time, we call it two‑dimensional motion. To describe it we need vectors – quantities with both magnitude and direction.

  • key Vector : displacement, velocity, acceleration, force.
  • key Scalar : speed, distance, time, mass.
  • Independence of motion – horizontal and vertical movements do not affect each other.
🌊 WOW: When you throw a ball, its horizontal speed stays almost constant (if we ignore air), while gravity pulls it down faster and faster.
📘 REMEMBER: Two‑dimensional motion = horizontal motion + vertical motion analysed separately.

2.2 Projectile motion – deep dive

A projectile is any object launched into the air and left to move under gravity alone. No engine, no wings.

Horizontal part

  • Velocity is constant (no horizontal force).
  • Horizontal distance = horizontal speed × time of flight.

Vertical part

  • Acceleration = g downward (about 9.8 m/s²).
  • Vertical velocity changes: decreases going up, increases going down.
  • Time to top = initial vertical speed ÷ g.

Key quantities (in words)

  • Time of flight – total time in air. Depends only on vertical motion and g.
  • Maximum height – reached when vertical speed becomes zero.
  • Range – total horizontal distance. Depends on horizontal speed and time of flight.
🏀 WOW: Without gravity, a basketball thrown would go in a straight line forever. Gravity bends the path into a parabola.

2.3 Uniform circular motion

An object moves in a circle at constant speed. Its velocity changes because direction changes – so it accelerates.

  • Centripetal acceleration – always points toward the centre.
  • Centripetal force – any force (tension, gravity, friction) that pulls inward.
  • Examples: a car turning a curve, Earth orbiting the Sun, a spinning fan blade.
📘 REMEMBER: In circular motion, speed may be constant, but velocity is not – so there is acceleration.

2.4 Rotational motion basics

Instead of metres and seconds, we describe rotation using angles.

  • Angular displacement – how many degrees or revolutions an object turns.
  • Angular velocity – how fast it rotates (revolutions per second or degrees per second).
  • Angular acceleration – change in angular velocity.
  • All points in a rigid rotating body have the same angular velocity, but different linear speeds (outer points move faster).
⚙️ WOW: The tip of a fan blade moves much faster than a point near the centre – but both complete one circle in the same time.

2.5 Torque – the twist that causes rotation

Torque is the turning effect of a force. It depends on:

  • How much force you apply.
  • How far from the pivot you apply it (lever arm).

Larger force or longer lever arm → larger torque.

Moment of inertia

Just as mass resists changes in straight‑line motion, moment of inertia resists changes in rotation. It depends on mass and how far it is from the axis.

Angular momentum

Rotational counterpart of momentum. Spinning objects keep spinning unless a torque acts. That’s why a bicycle wheel helps you balance.

📘 REMEMBER: Torque changes angular momentum – just as force changes linear momentum.

2.6 Universal gravitation & Kepler’s laws

Newton’s law: Every mass attracts every other mass with a force that is:

  • greater for larger masses,
  • smaller when distance increases (inverse square).

Kepler’s three laws (written simply)

  1. Planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus.
  2. A line from planet to Sun sweeps equal areas in equal times – so planets move faster when closer.
  3. The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the average distance from the Sun.

Satellites stay in orbit because gravity provides the centripetal force. They are always falling toward Earth but moving sideways so fast they keep missing.

🛰️ WOW: Astronauts feel weightless not because there’s no gravity, but because they are in free fall along with their spacecraft.

🚀 Where do we see these in real life?

  • Projectile motion – basketball shots, fireworks, sprinklers.
  • Circular motion – merry‑go‑round, artificial satellites, centrifuges.
  • Torque – opening a door, using a wrench, pedalling a bicycle.
  • Gravitation – tides, orbits, weighing objects.

📋 30 challenging questions (conceptual & problem‑solving)

All answers are written in plain language – no symbols, just clear physics reasoning.

1. A ball is thrown horizontally from a cliff. Does a heavier ball hit the ground sooner than a lighter one? Explain.
No. In the absence of air resistance, vertical acceleration is the same (g) for all masses. Both hit the ground at the same time.
2. Why does a projectile follow a curved path?
Because it has constant horizontal velocity while its vertical velocity changes uniformly due to gravity. The combination produces a parabola.
3. If you double the initial horizontal speed of a projectile (same vertical speed), what happens to its time of flight and range?
Time of flight depends only on vertical motion, so it stays the same. Range = horizontal speed × time, so range doubles.
4. A car rounds a curve at constant speed. Is it accelerating? Explain.
Yes. Acceleration is change in velocity (direction changes), even if speed is constant. The acceleration points toward the centre of the curve.
5. Why do astronauts inside the International Space Station float?
They are in free fall along with the station. Gravity still pulls them, but they have enough tangential speed to keep falling around Earth, creating apparent weightlessness.
6. What is torque, and how is it different from force?
Force causes linear acceleration. Torque causes angular acceleration. Torque depends on force and the distance from the pivot (lever arm).
7. Two satellites orbit Earth at different heights. Which one has a longer orbital period?
The satellite farther from Earth (higher altitude) has a longer period because gravity is weaker and it must travel a larger circle.
8. Explain why a figure skater spins faster when she pulls her arms in.
Conservation of angular momentum. When she pulls her arms in, her moment of inertia decreases, so her angular velocity increases.
9. Does the mass of a projectile affect its maximum height (ignoring air)?
No. Maximum height depends only on initial vertical velocity and g. Mass cancels out in the equations.
10. Why is it easier to close a heavy door by pushing at the edge rather than near the hinge?
Torque = force × distance from pivot. Pushing at the edge gives a larger lever arm, so more torque for the same force.
11. What would happen to Earth’s orbit if the Sun’s gravity suddenly vanished?
Earth would fly off in a straight line at the velocity it had at that moment (Newton’s first law).
12. In projectile motion, at which point is the speed minimum?
At the highest point, because vertical velocity becomes zero and only horizontal velocity remains.
13. A car moves around a roundabout. What provides the centripetal force?
Friction between the tyres and the road.
14. State Kepler’s second law in your own words.
A planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times – meaning it moves faster when closer to the sun.
15. If Earth were twice as massive, would your weight double? Explain.
Yes, because gravitational force is proportional to Earth’s mass. So weight (force on you) would double.
16. Why do projectiles launched at 45° have the maximum range (same initial speed)?
At 45°, the initial speed is evenly split between horizontal and vertical, giving the best combination of time of flight and horizontal speed.
17. Can an object have zero acceleration and still be moving? Give an example.
Yes. Constant velocity means zero acceleration. Example: a car cruising straight on a highway at steady speed.
18. In circular motion, is there a force pushing you outward? Explain.
No. The sensation of being pushed outward is inertia – your body wants to go straight, but the car seat pushes you inward (centripetal force).
19. A ball is thrown upward at an angle. Describe the direction of acceleration during the flight.
Always straight downward (toward Earth’s centre), constant magnitude g.
20. What is the difference between angular velocity and linear velocity?
Angular velocity measures how fast something rotates (angle per time). Linear velocity measures how fast a point moves along its path. For a given rotation, outer points have larger linear velocity.
21. If a planet’s distance from the sun is doubled, what happens to its orbital period (roughly)?
From Kepler’s third law, period increases by a factor of about 2.8 (square root of 2 cubed).
22. Why does a bicycle remain upright when moving but fall when stopped?
Angular momentum of the spinning wheels provides stability (gyroscopic effect), and also the rider makes small corrections.
23. A stone is whirled in a horizontal circle on a string. What happens if the string breaks?
The stone flies off in a straight line tangent to the circle (Newton’s first law).
24. Explain why the handle of a wrench is made long.
A longer handle increases the lever arm, so more torque is produced with the same force – makes turning easier.
25. What is the direction of centripetal force for a car turning left?
Toward the centre of the turn – so to the left (if turning left, centre is left).
26. If you fire a bullet horizontally and drop a bullet at the same time from the same height, which hits the ground first? (Ignore air)
Both hit at the same time. Gravity affects them equally in the vertical direction.
27. Why do astronauts in orbit feel weightless even though gravity is still strong?
Because they are in continuous free fall toward Earth. Their sensation of weight comes from the floor pushing up – in free fall there’s no push.
28. A force is applied perpendicular to a door at its edge. Does the door rotate faster if the same force is applied closer to the hinge? Explain.
No, it rotates slower because torque is smaller (smaller lever arm). Same force at the edge gives greater torque, so greater angular acceleration.
29. What is the only force acting on a satellite (like the Moon) after it is in orbit?
Gravity (from Earth or the Sun). In the vacuum of space, there is no air resistance or other forces.
30. (challenge) Imagine you throw a ball upward at an angle on the Moon, where g is one‑sixth of Earth’s. How do the time of flight and maximum height compare to Earth (same initial velocity)?
On the Moon, time of flight would be six times longer (because acceleration downward is smaller). Maximum height would also be six times higher.

💡 key insight

Two‑dimensional motion, rotation, and gravitation are everywhere – from sports to space. Understanding them deeply helps you see the world like a physicist.
