📘 Electronics – key ideas

Electronics studies the control of electrons in solids. Semiconductors (Si, Ge) have conductivity between conductors and insulators. Doping creates n‑type (extra electrons) and p‑type (holes). The p‑n junction forms diodes, which allow current in one direction. Diodes rectify AC, LEDs emit light, Zener diodes regulate voltage. Transistors (NPN, PNP) amplify and switch. Integrated circuits (ICs) pack millions of components. Logic gates (AND, OR, NOT) process binary data, forming the basis of computers.

📌 REMEMBER: Diode forward voltage ≈ 0.7 V (Si). Transistor β = IC/IB. LED current limiting resistor R = (Vsupply – VLED) / I.
✨ WOW: A modern CPU contains billions of transistors – each one is an electronic switch!
Q1 (conceptual)
Explain the difference between conductors, semiconductors, and insulators.
Answer:
Conductor: Many free electrons → high conductivity. Insulator: Very few free electrons → negligible conductivity. Semiconductor: Conductivity between conductor and insulator; it increases with doping or temperature.
Q2 (conceptual)
Why does intrinsic semiconductor conductivity increase with temperature?
Answer:
Thermal energy excites electrons from the valence band to the conduction band, creating more free electrons and holes. More charge carriers → higher conductivity.
Q3 (conceptual)
Explain the role of doping in semiconductors.
Answer:
Doping adds impurities to increase free carriers. n‑type: donor atoms (group V) provide extra electrons. p‑type: acceptor atoms (group III) create holes. This tailors conductivity for devices.
Q4 (conceptual)
Why is a diode considered a one‑way current device?
Answer:
A p‑n junction allows current only when forward‑biased (p positive, n negative). In reverse bias, the depletion layer widens and blocks current.
Q5 (conceptual)
Explain why LEDs emit light while normal diodes do not.
Answer:
In an LED, electron‑hole recombination releases energy as photons. The material is chosen so that the photon energy lies in the visible range. Normal diodes are made of silicon/germanium, where the energy is released as heat (infrared).
Q6 (conceptual)
What is the difference between forward bias and reverse bias in a diode?
Answer:
Forward bias: p‑side positive, n‑side negative → current flows. Reverse bias: p‑side negative, n‑side positive → current blocked (except small leakage).
Q7 (conceptual)
Why is a Zener diode used in voltage regulation?
Answer:
In reverse bias, Zener diodes break down at a precise voltage and maintain that voltage across them despite input fluctuations, providing stable output.
Q8 (conceptual)
Explain rectification using a diode.
Answer:
A diode converts AC to DC by allowing only one half‑cycle to pass. Half‑wave rectification uses one diode; full‑wave uses a bridge of four diodes to utilise both halves.
Q9 (conceptual)
Why are capacitors used in rectifier circuits?
Answer:
Capacitors smooth the pulsating DC output by storing charge when voltage rises and releasing it when voltage drops, reducing ripple.
Q10 (conceptual)
Explain how a transistor acts as an amplifier.
Answer:
A small base current controls a much larger collector current (IC = β IB). Thus a small input signal (varying IB) produces a large output signal across the collector resistor.
Q11 (conceptual)
Why is a transistor used as a switch in digital circuits?
Answer:
In saturation, collector‑emitter path acts as a closed switch (ON). In cutoff, it acts as an open switch (OFF). The base current controls these states.
Q12 (conceptual)
Explain the difference between NPN and PNP transistors.
Answer:
NPN: current flows from collector to emitter when base is positive relative to emitter. PNP: current flows from emitter to collector when base is negative. Their symbols and biasing voltages differ.
Q13 (conceptual)
Why are integrated circuits (ICs) preferred over discrete components?
Answer:
ICs are compact, low cost, reliable, and require less wiring. Many transistors, resistors, and capacitors are fabricated on one chip, reducing size and improving speed.
Q14 (conceptual)
Explain the logic behind AND, OR, and NOT gates.
Answer:
AND: output 1 only if all inputs are 1. OR: output 1 if at least one input is 1. NOT: output is the inverse of the input (1→0, 0→1).
Q15 (conceptual)
Why are combinational circuits faster than sequential circuits?
Answer:
Combinational circuits depend only on current inputs – no memory elements or feedback. Sequential circuits use previous states, which adds delay due to storage elements.
Q16 (conceptual)
Explain how a diode bridge converts AC to DC.
Answer:
Four diodes are arranged in a bridge. During the positive half‑cycle, two diodes conduct, passing current through the load in one direction. During the negative half‑cycle, the other two diodes conduct, again sending current through the load in the same direction – producing full‑wave rectification.
Q17 (conceptual)
Why is the forward voltage of a silicon diode approximately 0.7 V?
Answer:
The built‑in potential barrier of a silicon p‑n junction is about 0.7 V. This voltage must be overcome to allow significant current flow.
Q18 (conceptual)
Why does a transistor need biasing in amplifier circuits?
Answer:
Biasing sets the operating point (Q‑point) in the active region so that the input signal can be amplified without distortion (cutoff or saturation).
Q19 (conceptual)
Explain why a depletion layer forms at a p‑n junction.
Answer:
Electrons from n‑side and holes from p‑side diffuse across the junction and recombine, leaving behind ionised donor and acceptor atoms. This region is depleted of free carriers and acts as a barrier.
Q20 (conceptual)
Why is reverse saturation current very small in a diode?
Answer:
Reverse current is due to minority carriers drifting across the junction. Their concentration is very small, so the current is tiny (μA range) and temperature‑dependent.
Q21 (conceptual)
Explain the switching speed of a transistor.
Answer:
Switching speed is the time to turn ON and OFF. It depends on charge storage in the base region. Higher frequencies require faster transistors (e.g., RF transistors).
Q22 (conceptual)
Why do LEDs require current‑limiting resistors?
Answer:
LEDs have a very low resistance when forward‑biased. Without a resistor, the current would be excessive and destroy the LED. The resistor limits current to a safe value (e.g., 20 mA).
Q23 (conceptual)
Explain the difference between N‑channel and P‑channel MOSFETs.
Answer:
N‑channel MOSFET conducts when a positive voltage is applied to the gate relative to source; electrons form the channel. P‑channel conducts when gate is negative relative to source; holes form the channel.
Q24 (conceptual)
Why are logic gates implemented in CMOS technology?
Answer:
CMOS uses complementary n‑MOS and p‑MOS transistors, offering low power consumption, high noise immunity, and high speed – ideal for digital integrated circuits.
Q25 (conceptual)
Explain the significance of binary logic in electronics.
Answer:
Binary (1 and 0) represents ON/OFF states in digital systems. It is the foundation of computers, calculators, and all digital devices because it simplifies circuit design and signal processing.
Q26 (conceptual)
Why are diodes used in signal demodulation?
Answer:
In amplitude modulation (AM), a diode rectifies the modulated signal, removing one half and allowing a capacitor to extract the original audio (envelope detection).
Q27 (conceptual)
Explain why integrated circuits reduce noise.
Answer:
Short interconnects reduce parasitic inductance and capacitance. Components are close, so thermal and electromagnetic noise pickup is minimised, leading to more stable operation.
Q28 (conceptual)
Why is reverse recovery time important in fast diodes?
Answer:
Reverse recovery time is the time a diode takes to stop conducting after bias reversal. In high‑frequency switching circuits, a fast recovery time prevents power loss and overheating.
Q29 (conceptual)
Explain why transistor beta (β) is important.
Answer:
β = IC / IB (common‑emitter current gain). It determines the amplification factor of a transistor. A higher β means a smaller base current controls a larger collector current.
Q30 (conceptual)
Why are diodes, transistors, and ICs fundamental to modern electronics?
Answer:
They are the building blocks for rectification, amplification, switching, signal processing, and logic. Every electronic device – from smartphones to satellites – relies on them.
Q31 (calculation)
A diode has a forward voltage drop of 0.7 V and is in series with a 1 kΩ resistor and a 5 V supply. Find the current.
Step‑by‑step:
I = (V – Vd) / R = (5 – 0.7) / 1000 = 4.3 / 1000 = 4.3×10⁻³ A = 4.3 mA.
✅ Answer: 4.3 mA
Q32 (calculation)
A bridge rectifier is supplied with 10 V RMS AC. The load resistance is 500 Ω. Find the average DC voltage across the load (assuming ideal diodes).
Step‑by‑step:
For a full‑wave rectifier, VDC ≈ 0.9 × VRMS = 0.9 × 10 = 9 V.
✅ Answer: 9 V
Q33 (calculation)
An LED requires 2 V and 20 mA. It is powered by a 9 V battery. Find the series resistor value.
Step‑by‑step:
R = (Vsupply – VLED) / I = (9 – 2) / 0.02 = 7 / 0.02 = 350 Ω.
✅ Answer: 350 Ω
Q34 (calculation)
A transistor has β = 100 and a base current of 50 μA. Find the collector current.
Step‑by‑step:
IC = β IB = 100 × 50×10⁻⁶ = 5000×10⁻⁶ = 5×10⁻³ A = 5 mA.
✅ Answer: 5 mA
Q35 (calculation)
A forward‑biased silicon diode (0.7 V) is in series with a 1 kΩ resistor and a 12 V supply. Find the power dissipated in the diode.
Step‑by‑step:
I = (12 – 0.7) / 1000 = 11.3 / 1000 = 11.3 mA. Pdiode = Vd × I = 0.7 × 0.0113 = 7.91×10⁻³ W = 7.91 mW.
✅ Answer: 7.9 mW
Q36 (calculation)
A full‑wave rectifier has a 50 Ω load and an AC input of 10 V RMS. Find the peak current through the load (ideal diodes).
Step‑by‑step:
Vpeak = √2 × VRMS = 1.414 × 10 = 14.14 V. Ipeak = Vpeak / R = 14.14 / 50 = 0.2828 A ≈ 283 mA.
✅ Answer: 283 mA
Q37 (calculation)
A 100 μF capacitor is used to filter the output of a full‑wave rectifier with 50 Ω load and 10 V DC average. Estimate the ripple voltage (assume f = 100 Hz).
Step‑by‑step:
Iload = VDC / R = 10 / 50 = 0.2 A. Ripple voltage Vr ≈ Iload / (f C) = 0.2 / (100 × 100×10⁻⁶) = 0.2 / (0.01) = 20 V. (Note: This ripple is very high; a larger capacitor would be used to reduce it.)
✅ Answer: 20 V (estimation)
Q38 (calculation)
A transistor amplifier has an input signal of 10 mV and a voltage gain of 50. Find the output voltage.
Step‑by‑step:
Vout = gain × Vin = 50 × 10×10⁻³ = 0.5 V.
✅ Answer: 0.5 V
Q39 (calculation)
An n‑type semiconductor has electron concentration n = 10¹⁶ m⁻³, hole concentration p = 10¹⁰ m⁻³. Electron mobility μe = 0.14 m²/Vs, hole mobility μh = 0.05 m²/Vs, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C. Find the conductivity.
Step‑by‑step:
σ = e (n μe + p μh) = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × (10¹⁶×0.14 + 10¹⁰×0.05) = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × (1.4×10¹⁵ + 5×10⁸) ≈ 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 1.4×10¹⁵ = 2.24×10⁻⁴ S/m. The hole term is negligible.
✅ Answer: 2.24×10⁻⁴ S/m
Q40 (calculation)
A Zener diode has Vz = 6 V. It is used with a series resistor of 1 kΩ and a supply of 12 V. Find the current through the Zener.
Step‑by‑step:
I = (Vs – Vz) / R = (12 – 6) / 1000 = 6 / 1000 = 6 mA.
✅ Answer: 6 mA
Q41 (calculation)
In a transistor switch, VCC = 12 V, RC = 1 kΩ, and IC = 5 mA. Find the collector voltage.
Step‑by‑step:
VC = VCC – IC RC = 12 – 0.005 × 1000 = 12 – 5 = 7 V.
✅ Answer: 7 V
Q42 (calculation)
Two resistors 2 kΩ and 3 kΩ are in series with a diode (0.7 V) and a 12 V supply. Find the current.
Step‑by‑step:
Rtotal = 2000 + 3000 = 5000 Ω. I = (12 – 0.7) / 5000 = 11.3 / 5000 = 2.26×10⁻³ A = 2.26 mA.
✅ Answer: 2.26 mA
Q43 (calculation)
An OR gate has inputs 0 and 1. What is the output?
Answer:
OR gate output is 1 if at least one input is 1. So 0 OR 1 = 1.
✅ Answer: 1
Q44 (calculation)
An AND gate has inputs 1 and 0. What is the output?
Answer:
AND gate output is 1 only if all inputs are 1. 1 AND 0 = 0.
✅ Answer: 0
Q45 (calculation)
A NOT gate has input 0. What is the output?
Answer:
NOT inverts the input: NOT 0 = 1.
✅ Answer: 1
Q46 (calculation)
An NPN transistor has a base resistor of 1 kΩ, VBB = 5 V, VBE = 0.7 V. Find the base current.
Step‑by‑step:
IB = (VBB – VBE) / RB = (5 – 0.7) / 1000 = 4.3 / 1000 = 4.3 mA.
✅ Answer: 4.3 mA
Q47 (calculation)
For the same transistor, β = 100. Find the collector current.
Step‑by‑step:
IC = β IB = 100 × 4.3×10⁻³ = 0.43 A = 430 mA.
✅ Answer: 430 mA
Q48 (calculation)
A diode has a reverse current of 10 μA and a forward current of 20 mA. Find the ratio of forward to reverse current.
Step‑by‑step:
Ratio = 20 mA / 10 μA = 20×10⁻³ / 10×10⁻⁶ = 20×10⁻³ × 10⁵ = 2000.
✅ Answer: 2000
Q49 (calculation)
A full‑wave rectifier with a capacitor filter has AC input 15 V RMS, load resistance 100 Ω, and capacitor 470 μF. Estimate the DC output and ripple voltage (f = 100 Hz).
Step‑by‑step:
VDC ≈ 0.9 × 15 = 13.5 V. I = VDC / R = 13.5 / 100 = 0.135 A. Ripple Vr ≈ I / (f C) = 0.135 / (100 × 470×10⁻⁶) = 0.135 / 0.047 = 2.87 V.
✅ Answer: VDC ≈ 13.5 V, ripple ≈ 2.87 V
Q50 (calculation)
A diode follows I = IS (eV/VT – 1). If IS = 10⁻¹² A, VT = 0.026 V, and V = 0.7 V, find I (ignore the –1).
Step‑by‑step:
I ≈ IS eV/VT = 10⁻¹² × e0.7/0.026 = 10⁻¹² × e26.923 ≈ 10⁻¹² × 4.96×10¹¹ = 0.496 A = 496 mA.
✅ Answer: 496 mA (approx)
Q51 (calculation)
An LED requires 2 V, 20 mA. It is powered from a 9 V battery. Find the series resistor.
Step‑by‑step:
R = (9 – 2) / 0.02 = 7 / 0.02 = 350 Ω.
✅ Answer: 350 Ω
Q52 (calculation)
A MOSFET used as a switch has RDS(on) = 10 Ω, current I = 0.5 A. Find the power dissipated.
Step‑by‑step:
P = I² R = (0.5)² × 10 = 0.25 × 10 = 2.5 W.
✅ Answer: 2.5 W
Q53 (calculation)
A transistor amplifier has input 5 mV and voltage gain 100. Find output voltage.
Step‑by‑step:
Vout = 5×10⁻³ × 100 = 0.5 V.
✅ Answer: 0.5 V
Q54 (calculation)
Add the binary numbers 1011 and 1101. Give the result in binary.
Step‑by‑step:
1011 + 1101 = (11 in decimal) + (13 in decimal) = 24 decimal = 11000 binary.
✅ Answer: 11000
Q55 (calculation)
A voltage divider has R₁ = 2 kΩ, R₂ = 3 kΩ, Vin = 12 V. Find Vout across R₂.
Step‑by‑step:
Vout = Vin × R₂ / (R₁ + R₂) = 12 × 3000 / 5000 = 12 × 0.6 = 7.2 V.
✅ Answer: 7.2 V
Q56 (calculation)
A transistor has β = 150 and base current IB = 20 μA. Find IC.
Step‑by‑step:
IC = 150 × 20×10⁻⁶ = 3000×10⁻⁶ = 3×10⁻³ A = 3 mA.
✅ Answer: 3 mA
Q57 (calculation)
A Zener diode has Vz = 5.6 V, supply 12 V, series resistor 470 Ω. Find the Zener current.
Step‑by‑step:
I = (12 – 5.6) / 470 = 6.4 / 470 = 0.01362 A = 13.62 mA.
✅ Answer: 13.6 mA
Q58 (calculation)
A resistor carries 5 mA and its value is 1 kΩ. Find the voltage across it.
Step‑by‑step:
V = I R = 0.005 × 1000 = 5 V.
✅ Answer: 5 V
Q59 (calculation)
A diode bridge is supplied with 20 V RMS AC. The load resistance is 200 Ω. Find the peak current through the load (ideal diodes).
Step‑by‑step:
Vpeak = √2 × 20 = 28.28 V. Ipeak = 28.28 / 200 = 0.1414 A = 141 mA.
✅ Answer: 141 mA
Q60 (conceptual)
Explain why electronics knowledge is essential in modern technology.
Answer:
Electronics is the foundation of all modern devices: communication (smartphones, radio), computing (CPUs, memory), automation (robotics, industrial control), consumer electronics (TVs, wearables), and medical equipment. Understanding electronics enables design, troubleshooting, and innovation in technology.
