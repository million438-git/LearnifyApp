📘 Electrostatics and circuits – key ideas

Electrostatics deals with forces, fields, and potentials from stationary charges. Coulomb’s law, electric field, and potential are fundamental. In electric circuits, charge flows due to potential difference. Ohm’s law, resistance (series/parallel), power, and capacitors are essential. Kirchhoff’s laws conserve charge and energy. These principles explain everything from lightning to household wiring.

📌 REMEMBER: F = k |q₁ q₂|/r², E = F/q, V = W/q, I = V/R, P = I²R = V²/R.
✨ WOW: The electric field inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is always zero – that's why you're safe inside a car during a lightning storm!
Q1 (conceptual)
Explain why like charges repel and unlike charges attract.
Answer:
Electric force is a vector along the line joining charges. According to Coulomb's law, F = k |q₁ q₂| / r². The sign of the product q₁ q₂ determines direction: same sign → repulsion; opposite signs → attraction.
Q2 (conceptual)
Why is the electric field a vector quantity?
Answer:
Electric field has both magnitude and direction (the direction of the force on a positive test charge). Therefore it is a vector.
Q3 (conceptual)
Explain why a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium has zero electric field inside.
Answer:
Free charges in the conductor redistribute until they cancel any internal field. If there were a field, charges would move; equilibrium means no net motion → E = 0 inside.
Q4 (conceptual)
Why does a charged object induce charge separation in a neutral conductor?
Answer:
The electric field of the charged object repels or attracts free electrons in the conductor, causing one side to become negative and the other positive – induced charge.
Q5 (conceptual)
Explain why potential difference drives current in a circuit.
Answer:
Voltage provides energy per unit charge, creating an electric field that pushes charges. Without potential difference, charges would not flow.
Q6 (conceptual)
Why is resistance of a wire proportional to its length?
Answer:
Longer wire means more collisions for electrons → higher opposition to flow → resistance increases with length.
Q7 (conceptual)
Why does resistance decrease with increasing cross-sectional area?
Answer:
More area provides more paths for electrons → easier flow → resistance decreases (R ∝ 1/A).
Q8 (conceptual)
Explain why insulators prevent charge flow.
Answer:
Electrons are tightly bound to atoms and cannot move freely → no charge flow (no current).
Q9 (conceptual)
Why does current split in parallel branches?
Answer:
Current divides inversely proportional to resistance; total current entering a junction equals sum leaving (Kirchhoff’s current law).
Q10 (conceptual)
Explain why series resistors share the same current but different voltages.
Answer:
In series, there’s only one path → same current through each. Voltage divides: V = I R, so larger resistance gets larger voltage.
Q11 (conceptual)
Why does the electric field between parallel plates remain uniform?
Answer:
For infinite parallel plates with equal and opposite charge, field lines are parallel and equally spaced → constant magnitude and direction.
Q12 (conceptual)
Explain why grounding neutralizes excess charge.
Answer:
Ground provides a large reservoir of charge. Electrons move to or from ground until the object reaches zero potential, neutralizing excess charge.
Q13 (conceptual)
Why is energy dissipated as heat in resistors?
Answer:
Electrons collide with atoms, transferring kinetic energy to thermal energy. Power dissipated = I² R.
Q14 (conceptual)
Why is a capacitor used to store charge?
Answer:
Capacitors store energy in the electric field between plates. Energy = ½ C V², and charge Q = C V can be released later.
Q15 (conceptual)
Why does current increase if voltage is increased in Ohm’s law?
Answer:
I = V / R, so for constant R, current is directly proportional to voltage. More voltage pushes more charge per second.
Q16 (numerical)
Two charges q₁ = 5 μC and q₂ = –3 μC are 2 m apart. Find the force between them (k = 9×10⁹ N m²/C²).
Step‑by‑step:
F = k |q₁ q₂| / r² = 9×10⁹ × (5×10⁻⁶ × 3×10⁻⁶) / 4 = 9×10⁹ × 15×10⁻¹² / 4 = (135×10⁻³) / 4 = 0.03375 N.
✅ Answer: 0.0338 N (attractive).
Q17 (numerical)
A 12 V battery is connected to a 4 Ω resistor. Find current and power.
Step‑by‑step:
I = V / R = 12 / 4 = 3 A.
P = I V = 3 × 12 = 36 W.
✅ Answer: 3 A, 36 W
Q18 (numerical)
Two resistors 6 Ω and 3 Ω are in series. Find total resistance and current for 18 V.
Step‑by‑step:
R_total = 6 + 3 = 9 Ω.
I = V / R_total = 18 / 9 = 2 A.
✅ Answer: 9 Ω, 2 A
Q19 (numerical)
Two resistors 6 Ω and 3 Ω are in parallel. Find total resistance.
Step‑by‑step:
1/R = 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2 → R = 2 Ω.
✅ Answer: 2 Ω
Q20 (numerical)
A 0.5 F capacitor is charged to 12 V. Find stored energy.
Step‑by‑step:
U = ½ C V² = 0.5 × 0.5 × 144 = 36 J.
✅ Answer: 36 J
Q21 (numerical)
A charge q = 2 μC experiences a force F = 0.1 N. Find the electric field.
Step‑by‑step:
E = F / q = 0.1 / (2×10⁻⁶) = 50,000 N/C.
✅ Answer: 5×10⁴ N/C
Q22 (numerical)
A 10 Ω resistor carries 2 A. Find voltage across it and power dissipated.
Step‑by‑step:
V = I R = 2 × 10 = 20 V.
P = I² R = 4 × 10 = 40 W.
✅ Answer: 20 V, 40 W
Q23 (numerical)
A 12 V battery connected to R₁ = 4 Ω and R₂ = 6 Ω in series. Find voltage across each resistor.
Step‑by‑step:
R_total = 4 + 6 = 10 Ω.
I = 12 / 10 = 1.2 A.
V₁ = I R₁ = 1.2 × 4 = 4.8 V.
V₂ = I R₂ = 1.2 × 6 = 7.2 V.
✅ Answer: 4.8 V, 7.2 V
Q24 (numerical)
Three resistors 2 Ω, 3 Ω, 6 Ω are in parallel. Find equivalent resistance.
Step‑by‑step:
1/R = 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = 3/6 + 2/6 + 1/6 = 6/6 = 1 → R = 1 Ω.
✅ Answer: 1 Ω
Q25 (numerical)
A particle with charge q = 4 μC is at a point where electric potential V = 500 V. Find its potential energy.
Step‑by‑step:
U = q V = 4×10⁻⁶ × 500 = 0.002 J.
✅ Answer: 2×10⁻³ J
Q26 (numerical)
A wire 10 m long, cross‑sectional area 0.001 m², resistivity ρ = 1.68×10⁻⁸ Ω·m. Find its resistance.
Step‑by‑step:
R = ρ L / A = 1.68×10⁻⁸ × 10 / 0.001 = 1.68×10⁻⁴ Ω.
✅ Answer: 1.68×10⁻⁴ Ω
Q27 (numerical)
A 12 V battery connected to 2 Ω, 3 Ω, and 6 Ω resistors in series. Find total power.
Step‑by‑step:
R_total = 2 + 3 + 6 = 11 Ω.
I = V / R_total = 12 / 11 ≈ 1.0909 A.
P = I² R_total = (1.0909)² × 11 ≈ 1.190 × 11 ≈ 13.09 W.
✅ Answer: about 13.1 W
Q28 (numerical)
A 2 μF capacitor is charged to 10 V. Find the charge stored.
Step‑by‑step:
Q = C V = 2×10⁻⁶ × 10 = 2×10⁻⁵ C.
✅ Answer: 20 μC
Q29 (numerical)
Two charges 3 μC and 6 μC separated by 0.5 m. Find the force between them (k = 9×10⁹).
Step‑by‑step:
F = k |q₁ q₂| / r² = 9×10⁹ × (3×10⁻⁶ × 6×10⁻⁶) / (0.5)² = 9×10⁹ × 18×10⁻¹² / 0.25 = 162×10⁻³ / 0.25 = 0.648 N.
✅ Answer: 0.648 N (repulsive because same sign)
Q30 (conceptual)
Explain why total current is conserved at junctions in a circuit.
Answer:
Kirchhoff’s first law (junction rule) states that the sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum leaving. This is a consequence of charge conservation – charge cannot accumulate at a junction.
