📘 Modern physics – key ideas

Modern physics revolutionised our understanding of the microscopic world. The photoelectric effect proved light’s particle nature (E = hf). Quantum mechanics introduced wave‑particle duality (λ = h/p), uncertainty principle, and discrete atomic levels. Nuclear physics explains radioactivity (α, β, γ), half‑life, fission, fusion, and mass‑energy equivalence (E = mc²). Relativity connects space, time, and gravity. These principles underpin all modern technology: semiconductors, lasers, medical imaging, and nuclear power.

📌 REMEMBER: Photoelectric effect: K_max = hf – φ. de Broglie: λ = h/p. Radioactive decay: N = N₀ e^(–λt), T½ = ln2/λ. Mass defect: ΔE = Δm c².
✨ WOW: The same quantum ideas that explain why the Sun shines also make your smartphone’s transistors possible!
Q1 (conceptual)
Explain why classical physics could not explain the photoelectric effect.
Answer:
Classical wave theory predicted that electron energy should depend on light intensity, not frequency. Observations showed that electrons are emitted only above a threshold frequency, and their maximum energy depends on frequency, not intensity – contradicting classical physics.
Q2 (conceptual)
Why does increasing light intensity not increase electron energy in the photoelectric effect?
Answer:
Photon energy depends on frequency: E = hf. Intensity increases the number of photons, not the energy per photon. Each electron is ejected by absorbing a single photon, so its kinetic energy depends on photon energy, not on how many photons there are.
Q3 (conceptual)
Explain the significance of Einstein’s equation for photoelectric effect.
Answer:
K_max = hf – φ shows that light behaves as particles (photons) and that the electron’s maximum kinetic energy is the photon energy minus the work function. This was a cornerstone of quantum theory.
Q4 (conceptual)
Why is there a stopping potential in photoelectric experiments?
Answer:
The stopping potential is the voltage needed to stop the fastest electrons. It measures the maximum kinetic energy of ejected electrons (e V_stop = K_max).
Q5 (conceptual)
Why are electrons emitted instantaneously for light above threshold frequency?
Answer:
Each photon transfers its energy directly to a single electron in a particle‑like interaction. There is no time lag – emission is immediate, supporting the particle nature of light.
Q6 (conceptual)
Explain why low‑frequency light cannot eject electrons regardless of intensity.
Answer:
Each photon has energy E = hf. If this energy is less than the work function φ, even a huge number of photons cannot give a single electron enough energy to escape – no emission occurs.
Q7 (conceptual)
Why does increasing intensity increase current in photoelectric effect?
Answer:
More photons per second mean more electrons ejected per second, so the photoelectric current (number of electrons per second) increases.
Q8 (conceptual)
Explain why X‑ray spectra have continuous and characteristic lines.
Answer:
The continuous part comes from bremsstrahlung (deceleration of electrons). The characteristic lines are due to electron transitions within atoms of the target, producing photons with specific energies.
Q9 (conceptual)
Why is energy quantized in atomic models?
Answer:
Electrons in atoms can only occupy certain discrete energy levels. When they jump between levels, they absorb or emit photons with energy equal to the difference, producing line spectra.
Q10 (conceptual)
Explain why Bohr model predicts hydrogen spectral lines.
Answer:
The Bohr model assumes electrons orbit in quantized angular momentum states. The energy difference between orbits gives the photon energy ΔE = hf, matching the observed hydrogen spectrum.
Q11 (conceptual)
Why can heavier elements not strictly follow Bohr’s model?
Answer:
Electron‑electron interactions become significant, and energy levels are not simply given by a one‑electron formula. A full quantum mechanical treatment (Schrödinger equation) is needed.
Q12 (conceptual)
Explain why Heisenberg uncertainty principle limits precision.
Answer:
The uncertainty principle states Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π. It is impossible to know both position and momentum of a particle simultaneously with arbitrary precision – a fundamental limit of quantum mechanics.
Q13 (conceptual)
Why does wave‑particle duality matter in modern physics?
Answer:
It explains that both light and matter (like electrons) exhibit wave and particle properties. This is the basis for electron diffraction, quantum mechanics, and the behavior of semiconductors.
Q14 (conceptual)
Why is de Broglie wavelength important for electrons?
Answer:
λ = h/p shows that electrons have a wavelength; this is confirmed by electron diffraction experiments, proving their wave nature.
Q15 (conceptual)
Explain why nuclear binding energy per nucleon varies across elements.
Answer:
It depends on the balance between strong nuclear force and Coulomb repulsion. Medium‑mass nuclei (like iron) have the highest binding energy per nucleon – they are the most stable.
Q16 (conceptual)
Why does radioactivity decrease exponentially over time?
Answer:
Each nucleus has a constant probability λ of decaying per unit time. This leads to the exponential law N(t) = N₀ e^(–λt).
Q17 (conceptual)
Why do alpha particles have low penetration?
Answer:
Alpha particles are heavy and doubly charged, so they lose energy rapidly through collisions with atoms – they are stopped by a sheet of paper or a few cm of air.
Q18 (conceptual)
Explain why beta particles are more penetrating than alpha.
Answer:
Beta particles are much lighter and faster, so they interact less frequently and can penetrate further (e.g., through a few mm of aluminium).
Q19 (conceptual)
Why are gamma rays highly penetrating?
Answer:
Gamma rays are electromagnetic radiation with no charge, so they interact weakly with matter. They can penetrate several cm of lead or metres of concrete.
Q20 (conceptual)
Explain half‑life concept.
Answer:
Half‑life is the time required for half the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. It is a statistical property, independent of the initial amount.
Q21 (conceptual)
Why is nuclear fission accompanied by huge energy release?
Answer:
A heavy nucleus splits into lighter, more stable nuclei. The mass of the products is less than the original mass; the mass defect is converted to energy according to E = Δm c² – a huge amount per reaction.
Q22 (conceptual)
Explain why chain reactions require critical mass.
Answer:
If the fissile material is below critical mass, too many neutrons escape without causing further fissions. Critical mass ensures enough neutrons are captured to sustain the chain reaction.
Q23 (conceptual)
Why does nuclear fusion occur at extremely high temperatures?
Answer:
High temperature gives nuclei enough kinetic energy to overcome the strong Coulomb repulsion between positively charged nuclei, allowing them to get close enough for the strong nuclear force to fuse them.
Q24 (conceptual)
Why are neutrinos emitted in beta decay?
Answer:
Neutrinos are emitted to conserve energy, momentum, and spin. The continuous beta spectrum showed that some energy was missing; the neutrino carries it away.
Q25 (conceptual)
Explain why mass of nucleus is less than sum of nucleons.
Answer:
The mass defect corresponds to the binding energy that holds the nucleus together. That energy (E = Δm c²) is released when nucleons combine.
Q26 (conceptual)
Why is Einstein’s mass‑energy equivalence important in nuclear physics?
Answer:
It explains the enormous energy released in fission and fusion, and is the basis for nuclear reactors, weapons, and understanding stellar processes.
Q27 (conceptual)
Explain why energy levels in atoms are discrete.
Answer:
Quantum mechanics shows that electrons in atoms can only have certain allowed energies. Transitions between these levels produce photons with specific energies – observed as spectral lines.
Q28 (conceptual)
Why does pair production require photon energy greater than 1.022 MeV?
Answer:
Pair production creates an electron‑positron pair. Their total rest energy is 2 × 0.511 MeV = 1.022 MeV. The photon must supply at least this energy; any excess becomes kinetic energy.
Q29 (conceptual)
Why is annihilation of an electron‑positron pair accompanied by gamma rays?
Answer:
When matter and antimatter meet, they annihilate. Their total mass is converted to photon energy (E = mc²). Two gamma photons (usually 0.511 MeV each) are emitted to conserve momentum.
Q30 (conceptual)
Explain why understanding modern physics is crucial for technology.
Answer:
Modern physics is the foundation of semiconductors (transistors, chips), lasers, nuclear power, medical imaging (PET, MRI), and quantum computing. It drives innovation in nearly every high‑tech field.
Q31 (calculation)
A photon of wavelength 500 nm hits a metal with work function 2.5 eV. Find the maximum kinetic energy of ejected electrons. (h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s, c = 3×10⁸ m/s, 1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J)
Step‑by‑step:
Photon energy E = hc/λ = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸ / (500×10⁻⁹) = 1.9878×10⁻²⁵ / 5×10⁻⁷ = 3.9756×10⁻¹⁹ J. In eV: 3.9756×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 2.48 eV. Work function φ = 2.5 eV. Since 2.48 eV < 2.5 eV, no electrons are ejected. K_max = 0.
✅ Answer: 0 eV
Q32 (calculation)
A photon of wavelength 400 nm hits a metal with work function 2 eV. Find the maximum kinetic energy of ejected electrons (in eV).
Step‑by‑step:
E = hc/λ = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸ / (400×10⁻⁹) = 1.9878×10⁻²⁵ / 4×10⁻⁷ = 4.9695×10⁻¹⁹ J. In eV: 4.9695×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 3.106 eV. K_max = E – φ = 3.106 – 2 = 1.106 eV.
✅ Answer: 1.11 eV
Q33 (calculation)
An electron is accelerated through 500 V. Find its speed. (m_e = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C)
Step‑by‑step:
Kinetic energy K = e V = 500 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 8.0×10⁻¹⁷ J. v = √(2K/m) = √(2×8.0×10⁻¹⁷ / 9.11×10⁻³¹) = √(1.6×10⁻¹⁶ / 9.11×10⁻³¹) = √(1.756×10¹⁴) ≈ 1.325×10⁷ m/s.
✅ Answer: 1.33×10⁷ m/s
Q34 (calculation)
A uranium‑235 nucleus releases 200 MeV when it fissions. Convert this energy to joules.
Step‑by‑step:
1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J, so 1 MeV = 1.6×10⁻¹³ J. 200 MeV = 200 × 1.6×10⁻¹³ = 3.2×10⁻¹¹ J.
✅ Answer: 3.2×10⁻¹¹ J
Q35 (calculation)
The half‑life of carbon‑14 is 5730 years. What fraction of a sample remains after 17,190 years?
Step‑by‑step:
Number of half‑lives = 17190 / 5730 = 3. Fraction remaining = (1/2)^3 = 1/8 = 0.125.
✅ Answer: 1/8 or 0.125
Q36 (calculation)
A photon has energy 3 eV. Find its wavelength in nm. (h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s, c = 3×10⁸ m/s, 1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J)
Step‑by‑step:
E = 3 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 4.8×10⁻¹⁹ J. λ = hc / E = (6.626×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸) / 4.8×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.9878×10⁻²⁵ / 4.8×10⁻¹⁹ = 4.14×10⁻⁷ m = 414 nm.
✅ Answer: 414 nm
Q37 (calculation)
An electron has a de Broglie wavelength of 0.05 nm. Find its velocity. (m_e = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s)
Step‑by‑step:
λ = 0.05×10⁻⁹ = 5×10⁻¹¹ m. p = h/λ = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ / 5×10⁻¹¹ = 1.3252×10⁻²³ kg·m/s. v = p/m = 1.3252×10⁻²³ / 9.11×10⁻³¹ = 1.455×10⁷ m/s.
✅ Answer: 1.46×10⁷ m/s
Q38 (calculation)
A photon of energy 1 MeV attempts to produce an electron‑positron pair. Is it possible? Explain.
Step‑by‑step:
Electron rest energy = 0.511 MeV, positron rest energy = 0.511 MeV, total = 1.022 MeV. The photon has only 1 MeV < 1.022 MeV, so it cannot produce a pair. (It would need at least 1.022 MeV.)
✅ Answer: No, impossible.
Q39 (calculation)
A radioactive sample decays from 1000 to 125 in 3 half‑lives. Verify the numbers.
Step‑by‑step:
After 1 half‑life: 1000/2 = 500. After 2: 500/2 = 250. After 3: 250/2 = 125. Yes, it matches.
✅ Answer: Verified.
Q40 (calculation)
A nucleus has a mass defect of 0.16 u. Find its binding energy per nucleon if it has 20 nucleons. (1 u = 931.5 MeV/c²)
Step‑by‑step:
Total binding energy = 0.16 × 931.5 ≈ 149.0 MeV. Per nucleon = 149.0 / 20 = 7.45 MeV.
✅ Answer: 7.45 MeV/nucleon
Q41 (calculation)
A photon has wavelength 600 nm. Find its frequency.
Step‑by‑step:
f = c / λ = 3×10⁸ / (600×10⁻⁹) = 3×10⁸ / 6×10⁻⁷ = 5×10¹⁴ Hz.
✅ Answer: 5×10¹⁴ Hz
Q42 (calculation)
A gamma ray has frequency 1×10²⁰ Hz. Find its energy in keV. (h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s, 1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J)
Step‑by‑step:
E = hf = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ × 1×10²⁰ = 6.626×10⁻¹⁴ J. In eV: 6.626×10⁻¹⁴ / 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 4.14125×10⁵ eV = 414 keV.
✅ Answer: 414 keV
Q43 (calculation)
An electron is accelerated through 1000 V. Find its momentum. (m_e = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C)
Step‑by‑step:
K = e V = 1000 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.6×10⁻¹⁶ J. p = √(2 m K) = √(2 × 9.11×10⁻³¹ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁶) = √(2.9152×10⁻⁴⁶) = 5.40×10⁻²³ kg·m/s.
✅ Answer: 5.40×10⁻²³ kg·m/s
Q44 (calculation)
The half‑life of a substance is 10 minutes. What fraction remains after 25 minutes?
Step‑by‑step:
Number of half‑lives = 25/10 = 2.5. Fraction = (1/2)^2.5 = 0.1768 (since 2^2.5 = 5.6568, so 1/5.6568 = 0.1768).
✅ Answer: about 0.177
Q45 (calculation)
1 gram of caesium‑137 (half‑life 30 years) decays. Find its activity. (Avogadro number = 6.022×10²³, atomic mass = 137 g/mol)
Step‑by‑step:
Number of atoms N = (1/137) × 6.022×10²³ = 4.395×10²¹ atoms. Decay constant λ = ln2 / T½ = 0.693 / (30×365.25×24×3600) = 0.693 / 9.467×10⁸ = 7.32×10⁻¹⁰ s⁻¹. Activity A = λ N = 7.32×10⁻¹⁰ × 4.395×10²¹ = 3.22×10¹² Bq.
✅ Answer: 3.22×10¹² Bq
Q46 (calculation)
A photon of energy 2 eV hits a metal with work function 1.5 eV. Find the maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons.
Step‑by‑step:
K_max = E – φ = 2 – 1.5 = 0.5 eV.
✅ Answer: 0.5 eV
Q47 (calculation)
An alpha particle (mass = 4 u) has kinetic energy 5 MeV. Find its speed. (1 u = 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg, 1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J)
Step‑by‑step:
Mass m = 4 × 1.66×10⁻²⁷ = 6.64×10⁻²⁷ kg. K = 5×10⁶ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 8.0×10⁻¹³ J. v = √(2K/m) = √(2×8.0×10⁻¹³ / 6.64×10⁻²⁷) = √(1.6×10⁻¹² / 6.64×10⁻²⁷) = √(2.41×10¹⁴) = 1.55×10⁷ m/s.
✅ Answer: 1.55×10⁷ m/s
Q48 (calculation)
An electron moves at 1×10⁶ m/s perpendicular to a 0.01 T magnetic field. Find the radius of its orbit. (m_e = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C)
Step‑by‑step:
r = m v / (q B) = (9.11×10⁻³¹ × 1×10⁶) / (1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 0.01) = 9.11×10⁻²⁵ / 1.6×10⁻²¹ = 5.69×10⁻⁴ m = 0.569 mm.
✅ Answer: 5.69×10⁻⁴ m
Q49 (calculation)
A photon of wavelength 250 nm hits a metal with work function 3 eV. Find the maximum kinetic energy of ejected electrons.
Step‑by‑step:
E = hc/λ = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸ / (250×10⁻⁹) = 1.9878×10⁻²⁵ / 2.5×10⁻⁷ = 7.9512×10⁻¹⁹ J. In eV: 7.9512×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 4.97 eV. K_max = 4.97 – 3 = 1.97 eV.
✅ Answer: 1.97 eV
Q50 (calculation)
A sample initially has 10¹² atoms with half‑life 8 hours. How many remain after 24 hours?
Step‑by‑step:
24 h / 8 h = 3 half‑lives. N = 10¹² × (1/2)³ = 10¹² / 8 = 1.25×10¹¹.
✅ Answer: 1.25×10¹¹
Q51 (calculation)
In an electron diffraction experiment, the slit spacing is 0.2 nm and the electron wavelength is 0.05 nm. Find the angle of the first maximum.
Step‑by‑step:
nλ = d sinθ → for n=1, sinθ = λ/d = 0.05 / 0.2 = 0.25. θ = sin⁻¹(0.25) ≈ 14.5°.
✅ Answer: 14.5°
Q52 (calculation)
A photon of energy 1 MeV attempts to produce an electron‑positron pair in tungsten. Is it possible? Explain.
Step‑by‑step:
Minimum energy needed = 2 × 0.511 MeV = 1.022 MeV. 1 MeV < 1.022 MeV, so it cannot produce a pair (even in a material).
✅ Answer: No.
Q53 (calculation)
An electron jumps between energy levels with ΔE = 3 eV. Find the wavelength of the emitted photon.
Step‑by‑step:
λ = hc / ΔE = (6.626×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸) / (3 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹) = 1.9878×10⁻²⁵ / 4.8×10⁻¹⁹ = 4.14×10⁻⁷ m = 414 nm.
✅ Answer: 414 nm
Q54 (calculation)
Activity 1 Ci = 3.7×10¹⁰ Bq. How many nuclei decay per second in a 1 Ci source?
Step‑by‑step:
1 Bq = 1 decay/s, so 1 Ci = 3.7×10¹⁰ decays/s.
✅ Answer: 3.7×10¹⁰ decays/s
Q55 (calculation)
An electron is accelerated through 100 V. Find its momentum. (m_e = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C)
Step‑by‑step:
K = e V = 100 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.6×10⁻¹⁷ J. p = √(2 m K) = √(2 × 9.11×10⁻³¹ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁷) = √(2.9152×10⁻⁴⁷) = 5.40×10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s.
✅ Answer: 5.40×10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s
Q56 (calculation)
A photon of energy 5 eV ejects an electron from a metal with work function 2 eV. Find the speed of the electron. (m_e = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, 1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J)
Step‑by‑step:
K = 5 – 2 = 3 eV = 3 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 4.8×10⁻¹⁹ J. v = √(2K/m) = √(2×4.8×10⁻¹⁹ / 9.11×10⁻³¹) = √(9.6×10⁻¹⁹ / 9.11×10⁻³¹) = √(1.054×10¹²) = 1.027×10⁶ m/s.
✅ Answer: 1.03×10⁶ m/s
Q57 (calculation)
A nuclear reaction releases 1.2 MeV. Convert this energy to joules.
Step‑by‑step:
1 MeV = 1.6×10⁻¹³ J, so 1.2 MeV = 1.2 × 1.6×10⁻¹³ = 1.92×10⁻¹³ J.
✅ Answer: 1.92×10⁻¹³ J
Q58 (calculation)
A photon has wavelength 450 nm. Find its frequency.
Step‑by‑step:
f = c / λ = 3×10⁸ / (450×10⁻⁹) = 3×10⁸ / 4.5×10⁻⁷ = 6.667×10¹⁴ Hz.
✅ Answer: 6.67×10¹⁴ Hz
Q59 (calculation)
In an electron diffraction experiment, slit spacing is 0.1 nm and electron wavelength is 0.05 nm. Find the angle of the first minimum.
Step‑by‑step:
For a single‑slit minimum, sinθ = λ / d = 0.05 / 0.1 = 0.5 → θ = 30°.
✅ Answer: 30°

Explain why modern physics principles are essential for students and engineers.

Modern physics provides the foundation for semiconductors, lasers, nuclear energy, medical imaging, and quantum computing. Understanding these principles enables innovation in electronics, energy, medicine, and materials science – the technologies that shape our world.
