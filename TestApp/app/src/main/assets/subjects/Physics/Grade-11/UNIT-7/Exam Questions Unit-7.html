📘 Nuclear physics – key ideas

The nucleus contains protons and neutrons bound by the strong nuclear force. Binding energy determines stability. Radioactive decay (alpha, beta, gamma) transforms nuclei. Half‑life quantifies decay rate. Fission splits heavy nuclei; fusion joins light nuclei – both release enormous energy. Nuclear reactions are used in power, medicine, and dating. Understanding these concepts is essential for modern physics and technology.

📌 REMEMBER: Binding energy per nucleon peaks around iron – most stable element. Energy released = mass defect × c² (E = Δm c²).
✨ WOW: The energy released in nuclear reactions comes from the conversion of a tiny amount of mass into energy – E = m c²!
Q1 (conceptual)
Explain why nuclear binding energy per nucleon determines the stability of a nucleus.
Answer:
Higher binding energy per nucleon means nucleons are more strongly bound → more stable. Lower binding energy → weakly bound → nucleus is prone to decay.
Q2 (conceptual)
Why does fusion release more energy per nucleon than fission for light nuclei?
Answer:
Light nuclei combine to form a nucleus with higher binding energy per nucleon → energy released. Fission of heavy nuclei also releases energy, but the gain per nucleon is smaller than for fusion of very light nuclei like hydrogen.
Q3 (conceptual)
Why is beta decay accompanied by an electron or positron?
Answer:
In beta minus decay, a neutron turns into a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino. In beta plus decay, a proton turns into a neutron, emitting a positron and a neutrino. This conserves charge and lepton number.
Q4 (conceptual)
Explain why alpha particles are highly ionizing but have low penetration.
Answer:
Alpha particles are massive and doubly charged, so they interact strongly with electrons in atoms → cause intense ionization. Their heavy mass makes them lose energy quickly → very short range (low penetration).
Q5 (conceptual)
Why is gamma radiation highly penetrating but weakly ionizing?
Answer:
Gamma rays have no mass and no charge, so they interact weakly with matter → low ionization. They can pass through thick materials → high penetration.
Q6 (conceptual)
Why does nuclear fission require a neutron to initiate?
Answer:
A neutron is uncharged, so it can penetrate the nucleus without being repelled by the positive charge. When absorbed, the nucleus becomes unstable and splits.
Q7 (conceptual)
Why is half‑life a crucial property of radioactive isotopes?
Answer:
Half‑life determines the rate of decay and allows us to predict how much of a sample remains after any given time. It is independent of the initial amount, making it useful for dating and medical applications.
Q8 (conceptual)
Explain why chain reactions require a critical mass.
Answer:
Below critical mass, too many neutrons escape without causing further fissions → chain reaction stops. Critical mass ensures that enough neutrons are captured to sustain the reaction.
Q9 (conceptual)
Why do alpha emitters become different elements?
Answer:
An alpha particle consists of 2 protons and 2 neutrons. When a nucleus emits an alpha, its atomic number decreases by 2 and mass number by 4 – it transforms into a different element.
Q10 (conceptual)
Why is carbon‑14 useful in dating ancient artifacts?
Answer:
Carbon‑14 is radioactive with a known half‑life (5730 years). While an organism is alive, it maintains a constant ratio of C‑14 to C‑12. After death, C‑14 decays without replenishment. Measuring the remaining C‑14 tells how long ago the organism died.
Q11 (conceptual)
Why are neutrons preferred for nuclear reactions rather than protons?
Answer:
Neutrons have no charge, so they are not repelled by the positively charged nucleus. Protons would need high energy to overcome Coulomb repulsion.
Q12 (conceptual)
Explain why fission reactors use moderators.
Answer:
Moderators slow down fast neutrons to thermal speeds. Slow neutrons are more likely to be captured by fissile nuclei like uranium‑235, sustaining the chain reaction.
Q13 (conceptual)
Why is the binding energy curve important in nuclear physics?
Answer:
The curve shows binding energy per nucleon vs. mass number. It explains why fission of heavy nuclei and fusion of light nuclei release energy – both processes move toward more tightly bound (higher binding energy) nuclei.
Q14 (conceptual)
Explain why radiation dose is measured in sieverts (Sv).
Answer:
The sievert accounts for the biological effect of different types of radiation, not just the energy absorbed. It weights the absorbed dose by factors that reflect how damaging each radiation type is to living tissue.
Q15 (conceptual)
Why is uranium‑235 preferred for nuclear reactors over uranium‑238?
Answer:
Uranium‑235 is fissile with thermal (slow) neutrons – it can sustain a chain reaction in a reactor. Uranium‑238 requires fast neutrons and is not easily fissionable by slow neutrons.
Q16 (numerical)
A nucleus has a mass defect Δm = 0.002 atomic mass units. Find its binding energy in MeV (1 u = 931 MeV).
Step‑by‑step:
E = Δm × 931 MeV = 0.002 × 931 = 1.862 MeV.
✅ Answer: about 1.86 MeV
Q17 (numerical)
Find the energy released when 0.01 kg of uranium‑235 undergoes complete fission. (Each fission releases 200 MeV; 1 u = 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg; Avogadro = 6.02×10²³)
Step‑by‑step:
Number of nuclei = mass / (mass per nucleus) = 0.01 / (235 × 1.66×10⁻²⁷) ≈ 2.56×10²² nuclei.
Energy in J per fission = 200 × 1.602×10⁻¹³ = 3.204×10⁻¹¹ J.
Total energy = 2.56×10²² × 3.204×10⁻¹¹ ≈ 8.2×10¹¹ J.
✅ Answer: about 8.2×10¹¹ J
Q18 (numerical)
The half‑life of carbon‑14 is 5730 years. How much remains from a 10 g sample after 17,190 years?
Step‑by‑step:
Number of half‑lives = 17190 / 5730 = 3.
Remaining mass = initial × (½)³ = 10 × 1/8 = 1.25 g.
✅ Answer: 1.25 g
Q19 (numerical)
A sample emits 4000 Bq initially. After one half‑life, what is its activity?
Step‑by‑step:
Activity = A₀ / 2 = 4000 / 2 = 2000 Bq.
✅ Answer: 2000 Bq
Q20 (numerical)
An alpha particle (mass 4 u) is emitted with kinetic energy 5 MeV. Find its velocity (1 u = 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg, 1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J).
Step‑by‑step:
Energy in J = 5 × 10⁶ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 8×10⁻¹³ J.
Mass = 4 × 1.66×10⁻²⁷ = 6.64×10⁻²⁷ kg.
v = √(2E/m) = √(2 × 8×10⁻¹³ / 6.64×10⁻²⁷) = √(16×10⁻¹³ / 6.64×10⁻²⁷) ≈ √(2.41×10¹⁴) ≈ 1.55×10⁷ m/s.
✅ Answer: about 1.55×10⁷ m/s
Q21 (numerical)
If each fission of uranium‑235 releases 2.5 neutrons on average, how many neutrons are released from 10²³ fissions?
Step‑by‑step:
Neutrons = number of fissions × neutrons per fission = 10²³ × 2.5 = 2.5×10²³ neutrons.
✅ Answer: 2.5×10²³ neutrons
Q22 (numerical)
Calculate the energy released per gram of uranium‑235 fission, assuming 200 MeV per fission. (Avogadro = 6.02×10²³, 1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J)
Step‑by‑step:
Molar mass of U‑235 = 235 g/mol → 1 g = 1/235 mol.
Number of nuclei = (1/235) × 6.02×10²³ ≈ 2.56×10²¹.
Energy per fission = 200 × 1.6×10⁻¹³ = 3.2×10⁻¹¹ J.
Total energy = 2.56×10²¹ × 3.2×10⁻¹¹ ≈ 8.19×10¹⁰ J.
✅ Answer: about 8.2×10¹⁰ J per gram
Q23 (numerical)
The half‑life of a sample is 10 days. Find its decay constant in day⁻¹.
Step‑by‑step:
λ = 0.693 / T½ = 0.693 / 10 = 0.0693 day⁻¹.
✅ Answer: 0.0693 day⁻¹
Q24 (numerical)
A sample of Ra‑226 decays from 16 g to 2 g in 1600 years. Find its half‑life.
Step‑by‑step:
N / N₀ = (½)^(t / T). Here 2/16 = 1/8 = (½)³, so 3 = t / T → T = t / 3 = 1600 / 3 ≈ 533.3 years.
✅ Answer: about 533 years
Q25 (numerical)
In a fusion reaction, 4 g of hydrogen convert to helium with a mass defect of 0.03 g. Find the energy released (c = 3×10⁸ m/s).
Step‑by‑step:
Δm = 0.03 g = 3×10⁻⁵ kg.
E = Δm c² = 3×10⁻⁵ × (3×10⁸)² = 3×10⁻⁵ × 9×10¹⁶ = 2.7×10¹² J.
✅ Answer: 2.7×10¹² J
Q26 (numerical)
An alpha particle is emitted from Po‑210 with Q = 5.3 MeV. Find its momentum (mass of alpha = 4 u, 1 u = 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg, 1 MeV = 1.602×10⁻¹³ J).
Step‑by‑step:
Energy in J = 5.3 × 1.602×10⁻¹³ ≈ 8.49×10⁻¹³ J.
Mass = 4 × 1.66×10⁻²⁷ = 6.64×10⁻²⁷ kg.
p = √(2 m E) = √(2 × 6.64×10⁻²⁷ × 8.49×10⁻¹³) = √(1.128×10⁻³⁸) ≈ 1.06×10⁻¹⁹ kg·m/s.
✅ Answer: about 1.06×10⁻¹⁹ kg·m/s
Q27 (numerical)
A carbon‑14 sample has an activity of 0.01 Bq. If the number of nuclei is 6.02×10²³, find the decay constant λ.
Step‑by‑step:
λ = A / N = 0.01 / 6.02×10²³ ≈ 1.66×10⁻²⁶ s⁻¹.
✅ Answer: 1.66×10⁻²⁶ s⁻¹
Q28 (numerical)
In a chain reaction, each fission releases 3 neutrons. If there are 10²³ fissile nuclei, what is the maximum possible number of neutrons released if all undergo fission?
Step‑by‑step:
Total neutrons = number of fissions × neutrons per fission = 10²³ × 3 = 3×10²³ neutrons.
✅ Answer: 3×10²³ neutrons
Q29 (numerical)
The fission of 1 kg of uranium‑235 releases 8×10¹³ J. How many fissions occurred? (Energy per fission = 200 MeV = 3.2×10⁻¹¹ J)
Step‑by‑step:
Number of fissions = total energy / energy per fission = 8×10¹³ / 3.2×10⁻¹¹ = 2.5×10²⁴ fissions.
✅ Answer: 2.5×10²⁴ fissions
Q30 (conceptual)
Explain why nuclear energy is both powerful and dangerous.
Answer:
Nuclear energy has an extremely high energy density – a small mass produces enormous energy (E = m c²). However, it is dangerous because of radiation hazards, the risk of meltdown, long‑lived radioactive waste, and potential for misuse in weapons.
Q31 (conceptual)
Why do heavy nuclei tend to undergo alpha decay?
Answer:
Heavy nuclei have many protons, causing strong Coulomb repulsion. Alpha decay reduces both proton and neutron numbers, moving towards a more stable configuration with higher binding energy per nucleon.
Q32 (conceptual)
Explain the difference between nuclear fission and nuclear fusion.
Answer:
Fission splits a heavy nucleus into lighter ones, releasing energy. Fusion combines very light nuclei into a heavier nucleus, also releasing energy. Both occur because the products have higher binding energy per nucleon than the reactants.
Q33 (conceptual)
Why is a chain reaction possible only with certain isotopes like uranium‑235?
Answer:
In fission, neutrons are released that can cause further fissions. U‑235 fissions easily with slow neutrons and releases enough neutrons to sustain a chain. U‑238 requires fast neutrons and produces fewer neutrons, making a chain reaction difficult.
Q34 (conceptual)
What is the role of control rods in a nuclear reactor?
Answer:
Control rods absorb neutrons. By inserting them deeper into the core, they reduce the number of neutrons available for fission, thus controlling the reaction rate and preventing overheating.
Q35 (conceptual)
Explain why the sun produces energy through fusion and not fission.
Answer:
The sun is composed mostly of hydrogen (light nuclei). Fusion of hydrogen into helium releases energy because the products are more tightly bound. Fission of heavy nuclei is not possible because heavy elements are scarce in the sun.
Q36 (conceptual)
Why does the binding energy per nucleon curve peak at iron‑56?
Answer:
Iron‑56 has the most stable configuration; nucleons are most tightly bound. For lighter nuclei, fusion increases binding energy; for heavier nuclei, fission increases binding energy. Iron is the end point of both processes.
Q37 (conceptual)
What is meant by ‘mass defect’ and how does it relate to nuclear stability?
Answer:
Mass defect is the difference between the mass of a nucleus and the sum of its constituent nucleons. This missing mass is converted to binding energy. Larger mass defect per nucleon means greater stability.
Q38 (conceptual)
Explain why gamma rays often accompany alpha and beta decay.
Answer:
After alpha or beta emission, the daughter nucleus may be left in an excited state. It de‑excites by emitting a gamma photon, carrying away excess energy without changing the number of protons or neutrons.
Q39 (conceptual)
Why are some isotopes radioactive while others are not?
Answer:
Radioactivity occurs when a nucleus has an unstable combination of protons and neutrons. Stable isotopes have a balance that minimizes energy; unstable ones decay to reach that balance.
Q40 (conceptual)
Explain the concept of ‘neutron moderation’ in nuclear reactors.
Answer:
Neutrons released from fission are fast‑moving. Moderators (like water or graphite) slow them down to thermal speeds, increasing the probability of inducing further fission in U‑235.
Q41 (conceptual)
Why does nuclear fusion require extremely high temperatures?
Answer:
Nuclei are positively charged and repel each other. High temperature gives them enough kinetic energy to overcome Coulomb repulsion and get close enough for the strong nuclear force to bind them.
Q42 (conceptual)
What is the significance of the ‘magic numbers’ in nuclear physics?
Answer:
Magic numbers (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) correspond to completely filled nuclear shells, making nuclei particularly stable (like noble gases in atoms). Such nuclei have higher binding energy.
Q43 (conceptual)
Explain why radioactive decay is a random process but has a predictable half‑life.
Answer:
Decay of any individual nucleus is random – we cannot predict when it will decay. However, for a large sample, the average behavior follows an exponential decay law, with a constant half‑life characteristic of the isotope.
Q44 (conceptual)
Why are beta particles emitted with a continuous energy spectrum?
Answer:
In beta decay, the energy released is shared between the beta particle and an antineutrino (or neutrino). Since the neutrino can carry variable amounts of energy, the beta spectrum is continuous.
Q45 (conceptual)
What is meant by ‘radioactive series’ and give an example.
Answer:
A radioactive series is a sequence of decays from a long‑lived parent nucleus to a stable end product. For example, uranium‑238 decays through many steps to stable lead‑206.
Q46 (numerical)
A radioactive sample has a half‑life of 6 hours. How long will it take for 7/8 of the sample to decay?
Step‑by‑step:
If 7/8 decays, 1/8 remains. (1/2)^n = 1/8 → n = 3 half‑lives. Time = 3 × 6 = 18 hours.
✅ Answer: 18 hours
Q47 (numerical)
The activity of a sample is 800 Bq. After 12 days, it becomes 100 Bq. Find its half‑life.
Step‑by‑step:
Activity ratio = 100/800 = 1/8 = (1/2)^n → n = 3 half‑lives. So 3 T½ = 12 days → T½ = 4 days.
✅ Answer: 4 days
Q48 (numerical)
Calculate the binding energy of helium‑4 nucleus if its mass is 4.002603 u. (Proton mass = 1.007825 u, neutron mass = 1.008665 u, 1 u = 931.5 MeV/c²)
Step‑by‑step:
Mass of constituents = 2×1.007825 + 2×1.008665 = 2.01565 + 2.01733 = 4.03298 u. Mass defect = 4.03298 – 4.002603 = 0.030377 u. Binding energy = 0.030377 × 931.5 ≈ 28.3 MeV.
✅ Answer: 28.3 MeV
Q49 (numerical)
A 1 mg sample of polonium‑210 has an activity of 166 GBq. Find its decay constant in s⁻¹. (Avogadro = 6.02×10²³, atomic mass = 210 g/mol)
Step‑by‑step:
Number of atoms N = (0.001 g / 210 g/mol) × 6.02×10²³ = (1/210000) × 6.02×10²³ ≈ 2.867×10¹⁸. A = 166×10⁹ Bq. λ = A / N = 1.66×10¹¹ / 2.867×10¹⁸ ≈ 5.79×10⁻⁸ s⁻¹.
✅ Answer: 5.79×10⁻⁸ s⁻¹
Q50 (numerical)
How much energy is released when 1 kg of deuterium undergoes fusion to form helium? (Mass of deuterium = 2.014102 u, mass of helium‑4 = 4.002603 u, 1 u = 931.5 MeV)
Step‑by‑step:
Reaction: 2²H → ⁴He. Mass of two deuterons = 2×2.014102 = 4.028204 u. Mass defect = 4.028204 – 4.002603 = 0.025601 u per reaction. Energy per reaction = 0.025601 × 931.5 ≈ 23.85 MeV = 3.82×10⁻¹² J. Number of reactions in 1 kg = (1000 g / 2 g/mol) × 6.02×10²³ = 500 × 6.02×10²³ = 3.01×10²⁶. Total energy = 3.01×10²⁶ × 3.82×10⁻¹² ≈ 1.15×10¹⁵ J.
✅ Answer: 1.15×10¹⁵ J
Q51 (numerical)
A sample contains 10¹⁰ atoms of a radioactive isotope with half‑life 5 minutes. What is its initial activity in Bq?
Step‑by‑step:
λ = 0.693 / (5×60) = 0.693 / 300 = 0.00231 s⁻¹. A = λ N = 0.00231 × 10¹⁰ = 2.31×10⁷ Bq.
✅ Answer: 2.31×10⁷ Bq
Q52 (numerical)
A gamma ray has energy 1.2 MeV. Find its wavelength in metres. (h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s, c = 3×10⁸ m/s, 1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J)
Step‑by‑step:
E = 1.2×10⁶ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.92×10⁻¹³ J. λ = hc / E = (6.626×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸) / 1.92×10⁻¹³ = 1.9878×10⁻²⁵ / 1.92×10⁻¹³ = 1.035×10⁻¹² m = 1.035 pm.
✅ Answer: 1.04×10⁻¹² m
Q53 (numerical)
If the decay constant of a radioactive substance is 0.02 s⁻¹, find its half‑life.
Step‑by‑step:
T½ = 0.693 / λ = 0.693 / 0.02 = 34.65 s.
✅ Answer: 34.7 s
Q54 (numerical)
A neutron‑induced fission of U‑235 releases 200 MeV. How many fissions are needed to produce 1 kWh of energy? (1 kWh = 3.6×10⁶ J, 1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J)
Step‑by‑step:
Energy per fission = 200 × 1.6×10⁻¹³ = 3.2×10⁻¹¹ J. Number of fissions = 3.6×10⁶ / 3.2×10⁻¹¹ = 1.125×10¹⁷.
✅ Answer: 1.13×10¹⁷ fissions
Q55 (numerical)
A sample of iodine‑131 has a half‑life of 8 days. After 24 days, what fraction remains?
Step‑by‑step:
Number of half‑lives = 24/8 = 3. Fraction remaining = (1/2)³ = 1/8.
✅ Answer: 1/8
Q56 (numerical)
Find the mass defect of oxygen‑16 if its nuclear mass is 15.994915 u. (Proton = 1.007825 u, neutron = 1.008665 u, O‑16 has 8p + 8n)
Step‑by‑step:
Mass of constituents = 8×1.007825 + 8×1.008665 = 8.0626 + 8.06932 = 16.13192 u. Mass defect = 16.13192 – 15.994915 = 0.137005 u.
✅ Answer: 0.1370 u
Q57 (numerical)
A radioactive sample decays from 5000 to 625 in 30 minutes. Find its half‑life.
Step‑by‑step:
Ratio = 625/5000 = 1/8 = (1/2)³, so 3 half‑lives = 30 min → T½ = 10 min.
✅ Answer: 10 minutes
Q58 (numerical)
How much energy is equivalent to the mass of an electron (9.11×10⁻³¹ kg)? (c = 3×10⁸ m/s)
Step‑by‑step:
E = m c² = 9.11×10⁻³¹ × (3×10⁸)² = 9.11×10⁻³¹ × 9×10¹⁶ = 8.199×10⁻¹⁴ J = 0.511 MeV.
✅ Answer: 8.20×10⁻¹⁴ J (0.511 MeV)
Q59 (numerical)
A sample of 10⁶ atoms has a half‑life of 2 hours. How many atoms remain after 6 hours?
Step‑by‑step:
Number of half‑lives = 6/2 = 3. Remaining = 10⁶ × (1/2)³ = 10⁶ / 8 = 1.25×10⁵.
✅ Answer: 1.25×10⁵ atoms
Q60 (conceptual)
Explain how nuclear physics contributes to medical diagnostics and treatment.
Answer:
Radioisotopes are used in imaging (PET scans, gamma cameras) and radiotherapy (cancer treatment). Tracers like technetium‑99m help diagnose organ function. Controlled radiation destroys tumor cells.
