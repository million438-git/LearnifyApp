📘 Heat and calorimetry – key concepts

Heat is energy transferred due to temperature difference. Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through a material without bulk motion. Calorimetry measures heat exchange using the principle of conservation of energy. Specific heat capacity, latent heat, and thermal conductivity are essential properties. Understanding these allows us to predict temperature changes, phase changes, and energy flow in real‑world systems.

📌 REMEMBER: Heat gained = heat lost (in an isolated system). Q = m c ΔT for temperature change, Q = m L for phase change.

Q1 (conceptual)
Explain why temperature is not the same as heat.
Answer:
Temperature = measure of average kinetic energy of particles → scalar.
Heat = energy transfer due to temperature difference → measured in joules.
Q2 (conceptual)
Why does a good conductor of heat reach thermal equilibrium faster than an insulator?
Answer:
High thermal conductivity → energy transfer faster → equilibrium reached sooner.
Q3 (conceptual)
Explain why specific heat capacity differs for different substances.
Answer:
Depends on mass of particles, bonding, and energy required to raise temperature per kg.
Q4 (conceptual)
Why is calorimetry used to measure heat transfer?
Answer:
It quantifies energy exchanged, allows determination of specific heat or latent heat.
Q5 (conceptual)
A metal rod is heated at one end. Why does heat flow to the other end?
Answer:
Heat flows from high to low temperature due to molecular vibration → conduction.
Q6 (conceptual)
Explain why adding water at 80°C to 20°C water results in an intermediate temperature.
Answer:
Energy conservation: Heat lost by hot = Heat gained by cold → final temp is between 20°C and 80°C.
Q7 (conceptual)
Why is heat transfer faster in metals than in wood?
Answer:
Metals have free electrons → conduct energy faster. Wood is insulating → slower conduction.
Q8 (conceptual)
Why is latent heat important in phase changes?
Answer:
Energy absorbed or released without temperature change → changes internal energy.
Q9 (conceptual)
Explain why a mixture of ice and water remains at 0°C until all ice melts.
Answer:
Energy goes into breaking bonds (latent heat) → no temperature change.
Q10 (conceptual)
Why does thermal expansion differ for solids, liquids, and gases?
Answer:
Depends on molecular spacing and strength of intermolecular forces.
Q11 (conceptual)
Explain why calorimeters must be insulated.
Answer:
To prevent energy loss to surroundings → accurate measurement.
Q12 (conceptual)
Why does a thin metal rod conduct heat faster than a thick wooden rod of same length?
Answer:
Higher thermal conductivity → faster transfer; thickness also matters but conductivity dominates.
Q13 (conceptual)
Explain the difference between conduction, convection, and radiation.
Answer:
Conduction: direct particle interaction.
Convection: bulk fluid motion.
Radiation: electromagnetic waves → no medium needed.
Q14 (conceptual)
Why does water have a high specific heat capacity?
Answer:
Hydrogen bonding → requires more energy to raise temperature.
Q15 (conceptual)
Why is it incorrect to say “hot objects contain heat”?
Answer:
Heat is energy in transit, not contained in object. Object contains internal energy.
Q16 (numerical)
500 g of water at 80°C is mixed with 300 g at 20°C. Find final temperature (ignore heat loss).
Step‑by‑step:
Heat lost by hot = Heat gained by cold
m₁ c (T₁ – T_f) = m₂ c (T_f – T₂)
0.5 × (80 – T_f) = 0.3 × (T_f – 20)
40 – 0.5 T_f = 0.3 T_f – 6
46 = 0.8 T_f → T_f = 57.5°C.
✅ Answer: 57.5°C
Q17 (numerical)
A 2 kg metal block at 150°C is dropped into 3 kg water at 30°C. Find equilibrium temperature. (c_metal = 400 J/kg°C, c_water = 4200 J/kg°C)
Step‑by‑step:
Heat lost by metal = Heat gained by water
m_m c_m (150 – T_f) = m_w c_w (T_f – 30)
2×400×(150 – T_f) = 3×4200×(T_f – 30)
800×(150 – T_f) = 12600×(T_f – 30)
120000 – 800 T_f = 12600 T_f – 378000
120000 + 378000 = 12600 T_f + 800 T_f
498000 = 13400 T_f → T_f ≈ 37.2°C.
✅ Answer: 37.2°C
Q18 (numerical)
Calculate heat required to raise 500 g water from 20°C to 80°C (c = 4200 J/kg°C).
Step‑by‑step:
Q = m c ΔT = 0.5 × 4200 × 60 = 126000 J.
✅ Answer: 126 kJ
Q19 (numerical)
Ice 100 g at 0°C is added to 200 g water at 30°C. Find final temperature. (L_f = 334 kJ/kg, c = 4200 J/kg°C)
Step‑by‑step:
Heat gained by ice = mass × L_f + mass × c × T_f
= 0.1×334000 + 0.1×4200×T_f = 33400 + 420 T_f.
Heat lost by water = 0.2×4200×(30 – T_f) = 840×(30 – T_f) = 25200 – 840 T_f.
Equate: 25200 – 840 T_f = 33400 + 420 T_f
25200 – 33400 = 840 T_f + 420 T_f
–8200 = 1260 T_f → T_f ≈ –6.5°C? That's impossible (below freezing). It means not all ice melts. We need to check if the water can melt all ice. Maximum heat water can give cooling to 0°C = 0.2×4200×30 = 25200 J. Heat needed to melt all ice = 0.1×334000 = 33400 J > 25200 J, so only part of ice melts. The mixture stays at 0°C.
✅ Answer: 0°C (some ice remains)
Q20 (numerical)
A metal rod 2 m long, area 0.01 m², k = 200 W/m°C, ΔT = 50°C across its ends. Find heat conducted per second.
Step‑by‑step:
Q/t = k A ΔT / L = 200 × 0.01 × 50 / 2 = 50 W.
✅ Answer: 50 W
Q21 (numerical)
A calorimeter absorbs 500 J. Water inside rises 5°C. Mass = 0.2 kg. Find specific heat of calorimeter.
Step‑by‑step:
Q = m_cal c_cal ΔT → c_cal = Q / (m_cal ΔT) = 500 / (0.2 × 5) = 500 J/kg°C.
✅ Answer: 500 J/kg°C
Q22 (numerical)
A 100 g ice cube melts in water. How much heat absorbed? (L_f = 334 kJ/kg)
Step‑by‑step:
Q = m L = 0.1 × 334000 = 33400 J.
✅ Answer: 33400 J
Q23 (numerical)
500 g water at 90°C loses 50,000 J. Find final temperature. (c = 4200 J/kg°C)
Step‑by‑step:
Q = m c ΔT → ΔT = Q / (m c) = 50000 / (0.5 × 4200) ≈ 23.8°C.
T_f = 90 – 23.8 ≈ 66.2°C.
✅ Answer: 66.2°C
Q24 (numerical)
Metal block mass 1 kg, c = 400 J/kg°C, loses 10,000 J. Find temperature change.
Step‑by‑step:
ΔT = Q / (m c) = 10000 / (1 × 400) = 25°C.
✅ Answer: 25°C
Q25 (numerical)
Water 2 kg is heated from 20°C to 100°C. Find energy needed. (c = 4200 J/kg°C)
Step‑by‑step:
Q = m c ΔT = 2 × 4200 × 80 = 672000 J = 672 kJ.
✅ Answer: 672 kJ
Q26 (numerical)
Ice 200 g melts in water. Find heat absorbed. (L_f = 334 kJ/kg)
Step‑by‑step:
Q = m L = 0.2 × 334000 = 66800 J.
✅ Answer: 66800 J
Q27 (numerical)
Calorimeter mass 0.1 kg, absorbs 500 J, ΔT = 5°C. Find its specific heat.
Step‑by‑step:
c = Q / (m ΔT) = 500 / (0.1 × 5) = 1000 J/kg°C.
✅ Answer: 1000 J/kg°C
Q28 (numerical)
A metal rod 1.5 m long, area 0.005 m², ΔT = 40°C, k = 150 W/m°C. Heat conducted per second?
Step‑by‑step:
Q/t = k A ΔT / L = 150 × 0.005 × 40 / 1.5 = 20 W.
✅ Answer: 20 W
Q29 (numerical)
200 g ice at 0°C added to 300 g water at 50°C. Find final temperature. (L_f = 334 kJ/kg, c = 4200 J/kg°C)
Step‑by‑step:
Heat to melt all ice = 0.2×334000 = 66800 J.
Heat water can give cooling to 0°C = 0.3×4200×50 = 63000 J < 66800 J, so not all ice melts. Final temperature = 0°C with some ice remaining.
✅ Answer: 0°C (ice‑water mixture)
Q30 (conceptual)
Explain why calorimetry is crucial for experimental physics.
Answer:
It measures energy exchange accurately, allows determination of specific heat, latent heat, and thermodynamic calculations. Essential for realistic, quantitative physics experiments.
