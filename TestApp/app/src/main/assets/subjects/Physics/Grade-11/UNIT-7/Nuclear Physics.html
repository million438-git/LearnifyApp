☢️ Introduction

✨ WOW: The nucleus is 100,000 times smaller than the atom, yet contains 99.9% of its mass! A teaspoon of neutron star material would weigh billions of tons – it’s like a giant nucleus.

Nuclear physics studies the properties and behaviour of atomic nuclei – protons and neutrons held together by the strong nuclear force. This unit covers:

  • Nuclear structure (isotopes, nuclear size)
  • Binding energy and mass defect
  • Radioactivity (alpha, beta, gamma) and decay law
  • Half‑life and activity
  • Nuclear reactions: fission and fusion
  • Applications (dating, medicine, power)

⚛️ 7.1 Nuclear Structure

Nucleons and isotopes

Atomic nuclei contain protons (positive) and neutrons (neutral), collectively called nucleons.

Atomic number Z = number of protons.
Mass number A = Z + N, where N = number of neutrons.

Isotopes have the same Z but different N (e.g., carbon‑12 and carbon‑14).

Nuclear radius: R = R₀ · A^(1/3) with R₀ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m (1.2 fm).

🔗 7.2 Binding Energy & Mass Defect

The mass of a nucleus is always less than the sum of the masses of its individual nucleons. This difference is called the mass defect (Δm).

Δm = Z·m_p + (A – Z)·m_n – M_nucleus

This missing mass is converted into energy that holds the nucleus together – the binding energy.

E_b = Δm · c² (where c = 3.0×10⁸ m/s)

In nuclear physics we often use atomic mass units (u): 1 u = 1.66054×10⁻²⁷ kg = 931.5 MeV/c².

Binding energy per nucleon = E_b / A. It is maximum around iron‑56 (≈8.8 MeV/nucleon) – the most stable nucleus.

Example: For helium‑4: m(⁴He) = 4.002603 u, m_p = 1.007276 u, m_n = 1.008665 u.
Δm = 2·1.007276 + 2·1.008665 – 4.002603 = 0.030376 u.
E_b = 0.030376 × 931.5 = 28.30 MeV. Per nucleon = 28.30/4 = 7.075 MeV.
✨ WOW: The binding energy curve explains why both fission and fusion release energy – moving towards iron increases stability.

💥 7.3 Radioactivity

Types of decay

  • Alpha (α): emission of a helium‑4 nucleus (2p,2n). Nucleus loses Z by 2, A by 4. Example: ²³⁸U → ²³⁴Th + α. Highly ionising, stopped by paper.
  • Beta minus (β⁻): neutron → proton + electron + antineutrino. Z increases by 1, A unchanged. Example: ⁶⁰Co → ⁶⁰Ni + e⁻ + ν̄. Medium penetration (few mm aluminium).
  • Beta plus (β⁺): proton → neutron + positron + neutrino. Z decreases by 1, A unchanged. Example: ¹¹C → ¹¹B + e⁺ + ν.
  • Gamma (γ): high‑energy photon from excited nucleus. No change in Z or A. Very penetrating (lead required).
✨ WOW: In beta decay, the energy spectrum is continuous – this puzzled physicists until Pauli proposed the neutrino, a nearly massless particle that carries away the missing energy.

⏳ 7.4 Decay Law & Half‑Life

The number of undecayed nuclei decreases exponentially:

N = N₀ e^(–λt), where λ is the decay constant.

Half‑life (T₁/₂) is the time for half the nuclei to decay:

T₁/₂ = ln 2 / λ (ln 2 ≈ 0.693).

Activity A = number of decays per second: A = λ N (unit: becquerel, Bq).

Mean life τ = 1/λ.

Example: If a sample decays to 1/8 in 30 years, number of half‑lives = 3 (since 1/2³ = 1/8). So T₁/₂ = 30/3 = 10 years.

⚡ 7.5 Nuclear Reactions

Fission

A heavy nucleus splits into two lighter nuclei, releasing neutrons and energy. Example: n + ²³⁵U → fragments (e.g., ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr) + 2‑3 n + about 200 MeV.

Fusion

Light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy. Example: ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV.

The energy released (Q‑value) is given by the mass difference: Q = (M_initial – M_final) c².

✨ WOW: The Sun fuses 600 million tons of hydrogen into helium every second – that's the energy that sustains life on Earth!

🔬 7.6 Applications

  • Carbon‑14 dating: measuring remaining ¹⁴C in organic remains to determine age (up to ~50,000 years).
  • Medical: radiotherapy (cobalt‑60), PET scans (positron emitters like ¹⁸F).
  • Nuclear power: controlled fission in reactors.
  • Smoke detectors: alpha source (americium‑241) ionises air.

📌 Key Equations

Mass defect: Δm = Z·m_p + (A‑Z)·m_n – M_nucleus

Binding energy: E_b = Δm·c² (1 u = 931.5 MeV)

Decay law: N = N₀ e^(–λt) ; T₁/₂ = ln 2 / λ

Activity: A = λ N

Reaction energy: Q = (M_initial – M_final) c²

🎯 40 Advanced Nuclear Physics Problems

Each answer includes the formula used and a step‑by‑step explanation.

Q1
Define mass defect and binding energy. Why does iron‑56 have the highest binding energy per nucleon?
Solution:
• Mass defect: The difference between the mass of a nucleus and the sum of the masses of its individual nucleons. It arises because some mass is converted into energy (binding energy) when nucleons join.
• Binding energy: The energy equivalent of the mass defect (E = Δm·c²). It is the energy needed to break the nucleus into separate nucleons.
• Iron‑56 has the maximum binding energy per nucleon because nuclear forces are saturated and Coulomb repulsion is balanced. Lighter nuclei gain energy by fusion; heavier by fission – iron is the most stable.
Q2
Calculate the mass defect and binding energy (in MeV) for helium‑4. Given: m(⁴He) = 4.002603 u, m_p = 1.007825 u, m_n = 1.008665 u, 1 u = 931.5 MeV/c².
Solution:
Step 1: Mass of 2 protons + 2 neutrons = 2×1.007825 + 2×1.008665 = 2.01565 + 2.01733 = 4.03298 u.
Step 2: Mass defect Δm = 4.03298 – 4.002603 = 0.030377 u.
Step 3: Binding energy = Δm × 931.5 = 0.030377 × 931.5 = 28.30 MeV.
Step 4: Per nucleon = 28.30 / 4 = 7.075 MeV.
Q3
Explain why heavy nuclei (like uranium) tend to undergo alpha decay rather than beta decay.
Solution:
Heavy nuclei have many protons, so Coulomb repulsion is strong. Emitting an alpha particle (2p,2n) reduces both Z and N, moving towards more stable region (higher binding per nucleon). Alpha decay becomes energetically favourable when the total mass decreases. Beta decay changes N/Z but doesn't reduce total nucleons as effectively; for very heavy nuclei, alpha decay is more likely.
Q4
A radioactive sample has a half‑life of 8 days. What fraction of the original sample remains after 24 days?
Solution:
Number of half‑lives = total time / T₁/₂ = 24 / 8 = 3.
Fraction remaining = (1/2)^n = (1/2)^3 = 1/8.
Answer: 1/8 of the original sample remains.
Q5
The activity of a sample is 1200 Bq. After 20 minutes, it becomes 300 Bq. Find the half‑life.
Solution:
Activity ratio A/A₀ = 300/1200 = 1/4 = (1/2)².
This means 2 half‑lives have elapsed. 2 × T₁/₂ = 20 min → T₁/₂ = 10 min.
Q6
Write the alpha decay equation for uranium‑238 and find its Q‑value. Masses: U‑238 = 238.050788 u, Th‑234 = 234.043601 u, He‑4 = 4.002603 u.
Solution:
Equation: ²³⁸U → ²³⁴Th + ⁴He.
Mass of reactants = 238.050788 u.
Mass of products = 234.043601 + 4.002603 = 238.046204 u.
Mass defect = 238.050788 – 238.046204 = 0.004584 u.
Q = Δm × 931.5 = 0.004584 × 931.5 = 4.27 MeV.
Q7
Distinguish between fission and fusion. Give one example of each.
Solution:
• Fission: A heavy nucleus splits into lighter nuclei, releasing neutrons and energy. Example: n + ²³⁵U → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3n + 200 MeV.
• Fusion: Light nuclei combine into a heavier nucleus, releasing energy. Example: ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV.
Q8
Explain the roles of moderators and control rods in a nuclear reactor.
Solution:
• Moderators (e.g., water, graphite) slow down fast neutrons to thermal speeds, increasing the probability of inducing fission in U‑235.
• Control rods (e.g., boron, cadmium) absorb neutrons. Inserting them reduces the number of neutrons available for fission, controlling the chain reaction.
Q9
Calculate the energy released in the fusion reaction ²H + ²H → ³He + n. Masses: ²H = 2.014102 u, ³He = 3.016029 u, n = 1.008665 u.
Solution:
Mass of reactants = 2 × 2.014102 = 4.028204 u.
Mass of products = 3.016029 + 1.008665 = 4.024694 u.
Mass defect = 4.028204 – 4.024694 = 0.00351 u.
Energy = 0.00351 × 931.5 = 3.27 MeV.
Q10
The half‑life of carbon‑14 is 5730 years. A bone sample has 25% of its original C‑14. How old is the bone?
Solution:
25% = 1/4 = (1/2)², so two half‑lives have passed.
Age = 2 × 5730 = 11,460 years.
Q11
What is a chain reaction? Explain the concept of critical mass.
Solution:
A chain reaction occurs when neutrons from a fission event cause further fissions, sustaining the process.
Critical mass is the minimum amount of fissile material needed such that, on average, at least one neutron per fission causes another fission. Below critical mass, too many neutrons escape, and the reaction dies out.
Q12
Why is the mass of a nucleus always less than the sum of its nucleons? What is this difference called?
Solution:
This is due to binding energy. When nucleons combine, some mass is converted into energy (E = Δm c²) that is released. The missing mass is the mass defect, and its energy equivalent is the binding energy.
Q13
Iodine‑131 has a half‑life of 8 days. How long will it take for its activity to fall to 1/16 of its initial value?
Solution:
1/16 = (1/2)⁴, so 4 half‑lives. Time = 4 × 8 = 32 days.
Q14
Compare alpha, beta, and gamma radiation in terms of nature, penetrating power, and ionising ability.
Solution:
• Alpha: helium nucleus (2p,2n). Highly ionising, low penetration (stopped by paper).
• Beta: electron (β⁻) or positron (β⁺). Medium ionising, stopped by a few mm of aluminium.
• Gamma: high‑energy photon. Weakly ionising, very penetrating (requires thick lead or concrete).
Q15
If a radioactive nucleus has a decay constant λ = 0.02 s⁻¹, find its half‑life and mean life.
Solution:
Half‑life T₁/₂ = ln 2 / λ = 0.693 / 0.02 = 34.65 s.
Mean life τ = 1/λ = 1 / 0.02 = 50 s.
Q16
Write the beta‑minus decay equation for cobalt‑60 (Z=27, A=60).
Solution:
⁶⁰Co → ⁶⁰Ni + e⁻ + ν̄ (antineutrino). A neutron converts to a proton, increasing Z from 27 to 28 (nickel).
Q17
Calculate the binding energy per nucleon for oxygen‑16. Masses: m(¹⁶O) = 15.994915 u, m_p = 1.007276 u, m_n = 1.008665 u.
Solution:
Mass of 8p + 8n = 8×1.007276 + 8×1.008665 = 8.058208 + 8.06932 = 16.127528 u.
Δm = 16.127528 – 15.994915 = 0.132613 u.
Total binding energy = 0.132613 × 931.5 = 123.5 MeV.
Per nucleon = 123.5 / 16 = 7.72 MeV.
Q18
Explain the principle of carbon‑14 dating. What assumption is made about atmospheric C‑14?
Solution:
Cosmic rays produce ¹⁴C in the atmosphere, which mixes with stable carbon. Living organisms maintain a constant ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio through exchange with the environment. After death, ¹⁴C decays (T₁/₂ = 5730 years) without replenishment. Measuring remaining ¹⁴C gives time since death.
Assumption: the atmospheric ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio has remained constant over time (calibrated using tree rings and other methods).
Q19
How much energy is released when 1 kg of uranium‑235 undergoes fission? Assume 200 MeV per fission.
Solution:
Number of atoms = (1000 g / 235 g/mol) × 6.022×10²³ = 2.562×10²⁴.
Energy per fission = 200 MeV = 200 × 1.602×10⁻¹³ J = 3.204×10⁻¹¹ J.
Total energy = 2.562×10²⁴ × 3.204×10⁻¹¹ = 8.21×10¹³ J.
Q20
A particle has a mass of 1.0 g. What is its energy equivalent in joules? (c = 3×10⁸ m/s)
Solution:
E = m c² = 0.001 kg × (3×10⁸)² = 0.001 × 9×10¹⁶ = 9×10¹³ J.
Q21
The mass of a nucleus is 7.5 u. If its binding energy is 60 MeV, find the mass defect.
Solution:
Δm = E_b / 931.5 = 60 / 931.5 = 0.0644 u.
Q22
Write the alpha decay equation for radium‑226 (Z=88). Identify the daughter.
Solution:
²²⁶Ra → ²²²Rn + ⁴He. Daughter is radon‑222 (Z=86).
Q23
A radioactive sample initially has 10¹² atoms. Its half‑life is 5 minutes. How many atoms remain after 15 minutes?
Solution:
15 min = 3 half‑lives. Remaining = 10¹² / 2³ = 10¹² / 8 = 1.25×10¹¹ atoms.
Q24
Why do gamma rays often accompany alpha or beta decay?
Solution:
After alpha or beta decay, the daughter nucleus may be left in an excited state. It then de‑excites by emitting a gamma photon, carrying away excess energy without changing Z or A.
Q25
Calculate the decay constant of cobalt‑60 if its half‑life is 5.27 years. (1 year = 3.156×10⁷ s)
Solution:
T₁/₂ = 5.27 × 3.156×10⁷ = 1.663×10⁸ s.
λ = 0.693 / T₁/₂ = 0.693 / 1.663×10⁸ = 4.17×10⁻⁹ s⁻¹.
Q26
How many fissions per second are needed to produce 1 MW of power? (Energy per fission = 200 MeV)
Solution:
200 MeV = 200 × 1.602×10⁻¹³ = 3.204×10⁻¹¹ J.
1 MW = 1×10⁶ J/s.
Number = 1×10⁶ / 3.204×10⁻¹¹ = 3.12×10¹⁶ fissions/s.
Q27
Define the Q‑value of a nuclear reaction. How is it related to mass difference?
Solution:
Q‑value is the energy released (or absorbed) in a nuclear reaction. It equals (initial total mass – final total mass) × c². If Q > 0, reaction is exothermic; if Q < 0, endothermic.
Q28
Calculate the energy released in the reaction: ⁷Li + p → ⁴He + ⁴He. Masses: ⁷Li = 7.016003 u, p = 1.007825 u, ⁴He = 4.002603 u.
Solution:
M_initial = 7.016003 + 1.007825 = 8.023828 u.
M_final = 2×4.002603 = 8.005206 u.
Δm = 0.018622 u.
Energy = 0.018622 × 931.5 = 17.35 MeV.
Q29
Why is lead often used as shielding against gamma radiation?
Solution:
Lead has high atomic number (Z=82) and high density. Gamma rays interact via photoelectric effect and Compton scattering, both of which increase with Z. High density means more atoms per volume, increasing attenuation. Hence lead effectively absorbs gamma radiation.
Q30
A radioactive sample has a half‑life of 12 hours. What fraction remains after 48 hours?
Solution:
48 hours / 12 hours = 4 half‑lives. Fraction = (1/2)⁴ = 1/16.
Q31
Explain the concept of 'magic numbers' in nuclear physics. Give two examples.
Solution:
Magic numbers are numbers of nucleons (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) that correspond to completely filled nuclear shells, making nuclei exceptionally stable. Examples: ⁴He (2p,2n), ¹⁶O (8p,8n), ²⁰⁸Pb (82p,126n).
Q32
A sample contains 1 μg of pure ²³⁹Pu. Find its activity. (T₁/₂ = 24,100 years, atomic mass = 239.05 g/mol, Avogadro = 6.022×10²³)
Solution:
Number of atoms N = (1×10⁻⁶ g / 239.05 g/mol) × 6.022×10²³ = 2.518×10¹⁵ atoms.
T₁/₂ in seconds = 24,100 × 365 × 86400 = 7.60×10¹¹ s.
λ = 0.693 / 7.60×10¹¹ = 9.12×10⁻¹³ s⁻¹.
Activity A = λ N = 9.12×10⁻¹³ × 2.518×10¹⁵ = 2296 Bq.
Q33
Why does the binding energy per nucleon peak around iron? What does this imply for fission and fusion?
Solution:
The peak at iron means nuclei near iron are most stable. Lighter nuclei increase binding per nucleon when fused; heavier nuclei increase binding when fissioned. Therefore both fusion (of light nuclei) and fission (of heavy nuclei) release energy, moving towards iron.
Q34
If each fission of uranium‑235 releases 2.5 neutrons on average, how many neutrons are released from 1 kg of U‑235 undergoing complete fission?
Solution:
From Q19, number of atoms in 1 kg = 2.562×10²⁴. Neutrons = 2.562×10²⁴ × 2.5 = 6.405×10²⁴.
Q35
Write the beta‑plus decay equation for carbon‑11 (Z=6). Identify the daughter.
Solution:
¹¹C → ¹¹B + e⁺ + ν. A proton converts to a neutron, so Z decreases from 6 to 5 (boron‑11).
Q36
Calculate the energy equivalent of 1 atomic mass unit in joules.
Solution:
1 u = 1.66054×10⁻²⁷ kg. E = m c² = 1.66054×10⁻²⁷ × (2.99792×10⁸)² = 1.4924×10⁻¹⁰ J.
Q37
A particle of mass 2.5 u is found to have a binding energy of 20 MeV. What is the mass defect?
Solution:
Δm = E_b / 931.5 = 20 / 931.5 = 0.02147 u.
Q38
Explain how a smoke detector uses an alpha source.
Solution:
A smoke detector contains americium‑241, an alpha emitter. Alpha particles ionise air between two electrodes, creating a small current. When smoke enters, it attaches to ions, reducing current and triggering the alarm.
Q39
The activity of a radioactive sample is 800 Bq. After 12 days, it becomes 200 Bq. Find its half‑life.
Solution:
Activity ratio = 200/800 = 1/4 = (1/2)² → 2 half‑lives = 12 days → T₁/₂ = 6 days.
Q40
In the proton‑proton chain, four protons fuse into a helium nucleus. Given: mass of four protons = 4.031300 u, mass of ⁴He = 4.002603 u. Find the energy released per helium nucleus produced.
Solution:
Δm = 4.031300 – 4.002603 = 0.028697 u.
Energy = 0.028697 × 931.5 = 26.73 MeV.
