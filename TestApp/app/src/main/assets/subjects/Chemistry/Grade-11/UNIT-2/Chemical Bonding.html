and Molecular Structure · Class 11 Chemistry Chapter 4

🔹 Chemical Bond

Chemical bond: The force that holds different atoms in a molecule.

Octet Rule

Atoms take part in chemical combination to complete their octet or attain noble gas configuration.

Valence Electrons

Outermost shell electrons that take part in chemical combination.

Kossel's Facts

  • Highly electronegative halogens and electropositive alkali metals are separated by noble gases.
  • Anions and cations (halogens gain e⁻, alkali metals lose e⁻) acquire noble gas configuration.
  • Opposite charges stabilise by electrostatic attraction: e.g., Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl.

Modes of Chemical Combination

  • Electrovalent/ionic bond: complete transfer of e⁻ (metal + nonmetal).
  • Covalent bond: equal sharing of e⁻.
  • Co‑ordinate bond (dative): both electrons from one atom, shared by both.

⚡ Ionic or Electrovalent Bond

Formed by complete transfer of e⁻; electrostatic force between oppositely charged ions. e.g. NaCl:

[Fig: NaCl formation – Na → Na⁺ + e⁻, Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻; Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract]

MgO: Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻, O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻.

Electrovalency: number of electrons lost or gained during ionic bond formation.

Factors Affecting Ionic Bond Formation

  • Ionisation enthalpy: lower IE → easier cation formation → favours ionic bond (e.g., alkali metals).
  • Electron gain enthalpy: more negative → easier anion formation (halogens).
  • Lattice energy: energy released when ions come together. Higher lattice energy (smaller ions, higher charge) favours ionic bond.

General Characteristics of Ionic Compounds

  • Physical state: crystalline solids (crystal lattice), not single molecules.
  • Melting/boiling points: high due to strong interionic force.
  • Solubility: soluble in polar solvents (water), insoluble in organic solvents.
  • Electrical conductivity: poor in solid, good in molten state or in aqueous solution.
  • Ionic reactions: very fast in solution (e.g., Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓).

🔗 Covalent Bond – Lewis‑Langmuir Concept

Formed by mutual sharing of electrons between atoms.

  • Homoatomic: same atoms (H₂, O₂, Cl₂).
  • Heteroatomic: different atoms (HCl, CO₂).
[Lewis structures: H:H, Cl:Cl, etc.]

Lewis Representation (Lewis Structures)

  • Calculate total valence electrons.
  • Add electrons for anions, remove for cations.
  • Place shared pairs (single bond), then distribute remaining electrons to complete octet (lone pairs / multiple bonds).
[Examples: CO₃²⁻, O₃, etc.]

Formal Charge

Formal charge = valence e⁻ – (lone pair e⁻ + ½ bonding e⁻).

[Formal charge examples on O₃, NO₃⁻]

⚠️ Limitations of the Octet Rule

  • Incomplete octet: Li, Be, B compounds (LiCl, BeH₂, BF₃).
  • Odd‑electron molecules: NO, NO₂, ClO₂.
  • Expanded octet: PF₅, SF₆, H₂SO₄ (central atom >8 e⁻).
  • Other drawbacks: noble gas compounds (XeF₂), does not explain shape or energy.

📏 Bond Parameters

  • Bond length: equilibrium distance between nuclei (Å, pm).
  • Bond angle: angle between orbitals containing bonding electrons (determines shape).
  • Bond enthalpy: energy to break one mole of bonds (kJ mol⁻¹). Stronger bond → higher bond enthalpy.
  • Bond order: number of bonds between two atoms. H₂ bond order =1, O₂ =2, N₂ =3. Bond order ∝ bond enthalpy, ∝ 1/bond length.
Isoelectronic molecules have same bond order (e.g., N₂, CO, NO⁺ all bond order 3).

🌀 Resonance Structures

When a single Lewis structure cannot explain all properties, resonance hybrids are used. Example O₃:

[O₃ canonical structures: O–O=O ↔ O=O–O ; resonance hybrid]

More examples: CO₃²⁻, NO₃⁻, C₆H₆.

🧲 Polarity of Bonds

  • Non‑polar covalent: same atoms (H₂, Cl₂) – equal sharing.
  • Polar covalent: different electronegativity (HCl) – partial charges δ⁺ and δ⁻.

Dipole moment (μ) = charge × distance (Debye units).

Applications: determining polarity, molecular shapes (μ=0 → symmetrical; μ>0 → unsymmetrical), calculating % ionic character.

📐 VSEPR Theory

Postulates:

  • Shape depends on number of electron pairs (bonded + lone) around central atom.
  • Pairs repel; they arrange to minimise repulsion.
  • Multiple bonds treated as single super‑pair.
  • Lone pairs require more space and distort angles.
[VSEPR geometries: linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, etc.]

⚛️ Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

Developed by Heitler, London, Pauling. Covalent bond forms when orbitals of atoms with opposite spin overlap.

Orbital overlap concept: greater overlap → stronger bond.

[H₂ formation: overlap of 1s orbitals]

Types of Overlap: Sigma (σ) and Pi (π)

  • σ bond: end‑to‑end (axial) overlap – s‑s, s‑p, p‑p (along axis). Stronger.
  • π bond: sidewise (lateral) overlap of p orbitals; weaker.

🔄 Hybridisation

Mixing of atomic orbitals of slightly different energies to form new equivalent hybrid orbitals.

Features:

  • Number of hybrid orbitals = number of mixed atomic orbitals.
  • Orbitals of valence shell with almost equal energy take part.
  • Promotion not always necessary.
  • Hybrid orbitals are more effective in bond formation.

Types:

  • sp hybridisation: linear (180°) – BeCl₂, C₂H₂. 50% s, 50% p.
  • sp² hybridisation: trigonal planar (120°) – BF₃, C₂H₄. 33.3% s, 66.6% p.
  • sp³ hybridisation: tetrahedral (109.5°) – CH₄, NH₃, H₂O. 25% s, 75% p.

🌌 Molecular Orbital Theory (LCAO)

Atomic orbitals combine to form bonding (lower energy) and antibonding (higher energy) molecular orbitals.

Conditions for combination: similar energy, same symmetry, maximum overlap.

  • Bonding MO (σ, π): electron density increases between nuclei.
  • Antibonding MO (σ*, π*): node between nuclei.

Bond Order

Bond order = ½ (Nb – Na) [Nb = bonding e⁻, Na = antibonding e⁻]

Bond order > 0 → molecule stable. Higher bond order → stronger bond, shorter length.

Electronic configuration of some molecules

  • H₂ (σ1s)² → BO = 1, diamagnetic.
  • He₂ (σ1s)²(σ*1s)² → BO = 0, unstable.
  • O₂: BO = 2, paramagnetic (two unpaired e⁻ in π* orbitals).
[MO diagrams for H₂, He₂, O₂]

💧 Hydrogen Bonding

When H is bonded to highly electronegative atom (N, O, F), the H gains partial positive charge and attracts lone pair of another electronegative atom. It is weaker than covalent bond.

[H‑bond in HF: ··· H–F···H–F ]

Types:

  • Intermolecular H‑bond: between different molecules (H₂O, HF, NH₃).
  • Intramolecular H‑bond: within same molecule (o‑nitrophenol).
